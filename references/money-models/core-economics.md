## The Core Business Equation

Three metrics that triangle acquisition:
1. **CAC** (Cost to Acquire a Customer) -- how much it costs to get a customer
2. **Gross Profit (GP)** -- how much you make minus cost of delivery
3. **Payback Period** -- how fast you break even

### The Fundamental Rule
- **LTGP > CAC** at minimum 3:1 ratio
- LTGP = Lifetime Gross Profit
- If you don't have LTGP > CAC, you don't have a business

---

## CAC (Cost to Acquire a Customer)

### Definition
All costs required to sell a new customer: advertising dollars, media buyer payroll, creative team, software, sales commissions, salaries, managers of those teams.

### Calculation Examples

**Outreach model:**
- $3,000/mo emailer + $200/mo software + $800 commissions (8 sales x $100) = $4,000
- $4,000 / 8 sales = **$500 CAC**

**Content model:**
- $10,000/mo media payroll (2 people x $5K) + $1,000 commissions (10 x $100) = $11,000
- $11,000 / 10 sales = **$1,100 CAC**

**Paid ads model:**
- $4,000 media buyer + $20,000 ad spend + $10,000 commissions + $1,000 software = $35,000
- $35,000 / 10 sales = **$3,500 CAC**

### How to Improve CAC
1. Make better offers (higher response rates, higher conversion)
2. Lower CPMs (better offers get cheaper distribution)
3. Cheaper labor (less impactful lever)
4. Cheaper software (least impactful lever)
- Focus on offers and conversion -- orders of magnitude more impactful than cost-cutting

## The Two Most Powerful Words in Advertising

**Free** and **New**

---

## The Penny Gap (Dan Ariely / Hershey Kiss Test)
- 9x more people took a FREE Hershey kiss vs. a 1-cent kiss on a college campus
- "Penny gap" = the massive behavioral difference between free and any price

---

## The Chick-fil-A Model
Either it's **free** or it's **full price**. Never discounted. No in-between.

---

## Free vs. Discount Testing (Gym Launch Data)
- 20 locations tested: 10 used "free" in ads, 10 didn't
- Result: Close rates were IDENTICAL between free and non-free leads
- Only difference: cost per lead was lower for free
- Free group made MORE sales (same close rate, more leads)
- "Free brings broke people" is a myth -- rich people also like free stuff

---

## The Believability Test
- Famous marketer ran ad: "Give me $100 and I'll give you $1,000 back. Call this number."
- Nobody called. Too good to be true.
- $500/mo offer gets MORE optins than $5,000/mo offer because more people believe it

---

## Three Reasons People Don't Want Your Free Thing
1. They don't want IT (change what you're giving or how you describe it)
2. They don't believe you (too good to be true)
3. Wrong people seeing it (targeting issue)

---

## Create Flow, Monetize, Add Friction
- Step 1: **Create flow** -- give the big sexy thing away
- Step 2: **Monetize** -- sell the qualified people
- Step 3: **Add friction** -- optimize with filters

### Ways to Add Friction
- Types of questions
- Number of steps in a process
- Targeting
- Force consumption before next step
- Increase ad length

---

## Four Ways to Display Discounts
1. **Percentage off** -- "87% off the first visit"
2. **Absolute amount off** -- "$87 off"
3. **Relative equivalent** -- "less than going out to lunch"
4. **Discounted price next to retail** -- "$800 ~~$1,200~~"

### Portion Off (Subset of Discount Display)
- 12-month sub at $100/mo = $1,200 total
- 25% off = $300 off = 3 months free = $900
- All different ways of saying the SAME thing. Rotate monthly for ad freshness.

### Pro Tip on Discount Effectiveness
- For a discount to work, the market must already understand the commodity price
- "$29 chiropractic adjustment" works because people KNOW it costs more
- For intangible/digital products without market-known pricing, use percentages instead

---

## Pros of Discounts
- Compliant for cheap leads
- Collect some money upfront
- Exceptional for **two-step sales** -- get card on file, then upsell with "use the card you have on file?"

---

## Bottom Line on CAC
"If I had only one offer to make to convert or my family would be killed, it would be a free offer."

## FROM $100M LOST CHAPTERS

### 30D Cash as Core Metric
- **30D Cash** = gross profit extracted from a new customer in their first 30 days
- This is THE metric that determines scaling speed
- Why 30 days: every business can borrow money interest-free for 30 days via credit cards
- Cycle: charge card -> acquire customer -> collect 30D Cash -> pay card -> recycle -> acquire more

### CFA Levels Summary (from Book)
- **Level 1**: 30D GP < CAC -- floating on savings, slow growth, "where most businesses live"
- **Level 2**: 30D GP = CAC -- credit limit becomes ad budget, self-funding growth
- **Level 3**: 30D GP > 2x CAC -- each customer funds the next, doubling by customer not by month
- "The difference between Level 1 and Level 3 is the difference between hoping to grow and being unable to stop growing"

### Fastest movers are the best allocators: direct dollars first (vid: MPHn-nW30QU — "14 Years of Good Advice")

Speed in business isn't frantic energy; it's knowing the return on each dollar (ROIC). $5,000 on a website versus a 5-minute funnel with $5,000 of ads: ads can return $10K-$20K if they work, while a website only pays after something else happens.
When starting out, spend almost entirely on direct ways of making money: let people know, collect the money, make them happy enough to tell friends. Business selection matters too: construction, ecommerce inventory, and early software are cash constrained, while a solo service flows from day one.

### Subscription or one-time? Compare earnings per click (vid: I0nCuqu7Cac — "Hotline: mental toughness and offers")

Illustrative math from Hormozi, 100 clicks each. Subscription: 2% convert, $10 a month, 10% churn, so $100 LTV, $200 total, $2.00 EPC. One-time: 3% convert at $50, $150 total, $1.50 EPC.
The higher EPC wins only if you can fund the wait: here the subscription takes 10 months to match while the one-time collects five times the cash up front. Test both on your own traffic, then check cash flow before choosing. No cash to wait means reworking the money model.

### Inside an industry CAC is a commodity; retention is the edge (vid: 2nTkffKVnas — "12 rules of thumb, trades, learning")

Two look-alike agencies pay about the same to get a customer. One becomes many times more valuable because each customer is worth more, so it can outbid the other.
Example on a $100 a year customer: 50% annual retention means about $200 lifetime, 80% means about $500, so 30 points of retention is 2.5x the budget for acquisition. Measure the original January cohort a year later, not your total count. B2B target is above 80% annual.

### Revenue retention sets a business's ceiling (vid: DfXy_PzlC0o — "Get rich young, plus live Q&A")

Three states. Worst: last year's sales give you no credit this year, so growth only comes from selling more. Better: you keep last year's revenue and it stacks. Best: net negative churn. Logo retention can never top 100%, but revenue retention can when good customers spend more.
10% monthly churn loses about 70% of customers a year, so what you have at year end is roughly 30% of the base plus what you sold. That is why the fastest route to $1M (sell something once to many) is not the route to $100M: rebuilding that much sales every year means you cannot forecast payroll or make bigger bets.
Sticky vehicles are slower on the front end. Hard to get in is why it is hard to leave, so patience gets decided when you pick the vehicle.

### Turn a revenue goal into a daily outreach number (vid: WeH2E1fGgaw — "Daily Q&A: selling, comp and failure")

Goal of $1M a year means about $84,000 a month. Sell a $2,000 a month service to small businesses. Hormozi's rule of thumb for that buyer: about five months expected life, call it 20% churn, so LTV is $10,000.
$84,000 divided by $10,000 is 8.4 sales a month, call it three a week. At an assumed 100 attempts per appointment and one close in four, that is 400 per sale and 1,200 a week, or 200 a day over six days.
The point: connect the daily activity to the number, and the rest is math.

### Two kinds of logo churn, one tier ladder to beat both (extends revenue retention) (vid: 3fsJFUvA6Ts — "What Makes The Perfect Business")

Structural churn is people who move, die, or close; you cannot stop it. Voluntary churn is people who think you suck, and that is the one to fight. Give cheap customers a clear way to spend more: on a $9 and $99 ladder, lose 20% of the $9 members but move 10% up 11x and revenue retention tops 100%, so the business grows with no new sales. Hormozi credits John Paul DeJoria for the line: be in the resale business, not the sales business.

### Why revenue-based LTV fools you (vid: Q2VUuUGfpNY — "6 Hacks To Make More Money")

Extends LTGP. A $10 meal bought with $5 of ads looks 2:1, but with $9 delivery cost you spent $5 to make $1. And price moves it most: at $10 cost, going from $20 to $30 doubles gross profit, which few other levers can do.

### Three back-of-napkin LTV methods (vid: FXzDLLdxsCk — "Revenue without more marketing")

Lifetime: total sales over total customers; understates if growing. Churn: average monthly price over monthly churn; average recent months. Sales velocity: flat revenue over new sales per month; only works when revenue has held steady. The last is what he uses most on calls.

### Testing two prices on earnings per click (vid: TooAB8Ow6cQ — "3 Hours of Money Making Advice")

Extends comparing on EPC. Price divided by churn gives LTV. A caller's rough numbers: $77 at about 33% monthly churn is about $231; $137 at 50% is $274, roughly 20% more per buyer. So the lower price wins if it converts more than about 20% better. Price moves both conversion and churn, so plot each and take the max. If you lack the numbers, churned members' LTV is often available per member; average it and work back. Skool team's view after running the platform's data: 50% monthly is far too high, aim under 20%, good is 5 to 10%.

### Business genie: double one lever (vid: XwZH-lOKG9c — "Big 4 Customer Acquisition Models")

100 clients at $100 a month, 20% margin, $2K profit. Double new clients: profit doubles. Double purchases or lifespan: also doubles, but each customer is worth twice as much, so you can pay more to get them, making it the better business. Double price with the same $80 cost: $120 per client, six times the profit. Most owners rarely look at the price.

### LTV back to front, CAC front to back (vid: jzKpAtzKQ54 — "You NEED to Know This Number")

Build LTV from the most expensive thing backward so you stack the most gross profit per customer. Work CAC from the top of the funnel, because early percentages are small and can move by multiples: a better ad might take 1% click-through to 3%, while nothing triples a 60% show rate or a 35% close rate. Exception: a clear outlier, like a 5% show rate where the industry runs 60%, is a 12x sitting there. Rare, so most gains come from ads, offer and headline.

### Cookie math: a wide ratio lets you overpay (vid: jzKpAtzKQ54 — "You NEED to Know This Number")

Hormozi once ran local ads for a cookie company: free cookie with a beverage, leads under $1, one in ten came in, so about $10 to get someone through the door. Set beside his rough $14,000 Starbucks lifetime value in this video (he admits he doesn't know their CAC), CAC could go from $10 to $50 and still print. Costs rise as you hit colder audiences past $1M to $3M, and a wide ratio is what lets you absorb that. His own peak: first year of Gym Launch, $100,000 in, $10M back.
