## BILLING HACKS

### Bill Every 4 Weeks (Not Monthly)
- 4.3 weeks per month = 13 billing cycles per year (not 12)
- **8.3% more revenue** just by changing billing language
- Present as weekly ($50/week), bill every 4 weeks ($200)
- If you run 20% profit, this is a ~41% increase in profit
- No difference in take rates between "every 4 weeks" vs "monthly"

### 3% Processing Fee Hack
- Add "X + 3% processing fee" to every transaction
- Nobody declines over 3% processing
- Goes straight to bottom line
- If you run 10% profit, 3% added = 30% more profit

### Get Two Forms of Payment
- Offer to WAIVE the 3% processing fee if they provide a second form of payment
- Positions as benefit: "We pass those savings on to you"
- Reduces involuntary churn (expired cards, insufficient funds)
- 20% churn reduction from involuntary churn alone more than makes up the 3%

### Get ACH as Second Payment
- ACH = Automated Clearing House (bank-to-bank)
- Cheapest way to transact besides cash
- People rarely change bank accounts vs. credit cards
- Almost never declines
- Saves 1-2% on processing costs

## QUICK PAYBACK IMPROVEMENTS

### First and Last Month Today
- Collect 2 months upfront instead of 1
- Example: $160 CAC, add first-and-last = $160 collected upfront = 100% offset before 30-day recurring even hits

### Initiation/Onboarding/Enrollment/Activation Fee
- Made-up fee that is all gross profit
- Call it whatever sounds most natural for your business
- Pure margin, offsets CAC immediately

## CANCELLATION POLICIES

### Four Approaches
1. **Allow cancel during trial** -- increases sales, decreases stick
2. **Zero cancellations** -- decreases sales, increases stick (only works with premium customers)
3. **Prorated fee** -- bill them the difference of savings accrued
4. **One-time breakup fee** -- easiest to explain, increases both sales AND stick

### Best Practice: Make Cancellation Fee = Discount Given
- If they got $600 in discount, cancellation fee is $600
- Simple, everyone understands it
- Legally clean, ethically defensible

### Fee or Feedback
- Script: "I'll waive your cancellation fee if you sit down and tell me what we could do better"
- Let them vent. Be MORE upset than them about the problem.
- Try to solve it. If you can, offer a rollover upsell.
- Exit interview process: let them vent -> validate -> be more upset -> ask to make it up -> offer rollover/do-over

### Make It Easy to Cancel
- If customers have nowhere to complain inside your business, they WILL complain outside (one-star reviews)
- Clear cancellation process = fewer reviews, more save opportunities
- "Small businesses do not get rich by making stuff hard for their customers"

## INVOLUNTARY CHURN REDUCTION

### The Two-Payment Method
- Get card + ACH (or two cards) on file at signup
- When primary payment fails, auto-charge backup
- 20% of all churn is involuntary (expired cards, insufficient funds, bank holds)
- Fixing involuntary churn alone can reduce total churn by 20%
- This is the easiest churn fix because it requires zero product improvement

### ACH Benefits
- Bank accounts almost never change (vs. credit cards that expire every 3-4 years)
- Lower processing fees (saves 1-2%)
- Near-zero decline rates

## RETENTION PRICING TACTICS

### Lifetime Discount at Churn Point
- Amazon model: 5% off with continuity, 15% off if you stay past 5 months
- Place the incentive right AFTER your average churn month
- Two benefits: (1) people pay more upfront knowing it'll go down, (2) people stay longer to earn the discount
- Reminder cadence as they approach: "2 months left! 1 month left!"

### Big Head Long Tail
- Big upfront payment + small ongoing payments
- $5,000 down, $200/mo or $1,000 down then $19.99/mo
- Sunk cost fallacy prevents cancellation
- Very low cancellation rates
- People called before card expired to save their $19.99/mo rate

### Waived Fee for Commitment
- Charge a setup/initiation fee that's 3-5x monthly rate
- Waive it if they commit to continuity
- Creates urgency and perceived savings
- If they leave early, fee applies (= built-in cancellation penalty)

## CONTINUITY & RETENTION (Deep Pass)

### Four Ways to Apply "3 Months Free" Discount
1. **Upfront**: Free time first, paid term starts after. Pro tip: 3 free + 12 paid = 15 months total (NOT 3 free + 9 paid = 12)
2. **At the End**: Pay full year first, earn 3 free months. Encourages adherence
3. **Spread Over Term**: Divide discount across all payments (25% off/month)
4. **First + Last + Free**: Pay first and last month, free period between. Covers acquisition cost

### Gift Card Discount Hack
- Give discount as physical gift card redeemable after 3 payments
- Three outcomes: forget (full price), gift to friend (free referral), use it (still got 3 paid months first). All wins.

### Lifetime Discount at Churn Point
- Average drop-off at month 4? Offer lifetime discount at month 5
- Remind at months 2 and 3. Gets people to buy at higher rates AND stay longer

### Cancellation Framework
- Preferred: cancellation fee = discount they received
- "Fee or Feedback" -- waive fee for exit interview
- In exit interview: be MORE upset than them about their issue -> they calm you down -> you've won
- Exit interviews: over half get resold into a higher-level package
- "Small businesses do not get rich by making stuff hard for customers"

### Five Retention Strategies (Top 2% Gyms)
1. **Consumption accountability:** If they miss by Wednesday, escalate -- repeatedly contact to get them scheduled
2. **Exit interviews:** In-person conversation required to cancel. Collect data + resell
3. **Member events:** Quarterly community events minimum
4. **Personalized handwritten cards:** Must reference specific progress ("You went from black band to yellow band")
5. **Personal check-ins:** Text every 14-21 days, independent of service. Just "How's life?" NOT automated

### Automating What You Should Systematize Destroys Goodwill
- "As soon as someone finds out that communications are automated, they stop paying attention"
- Automated outreach WITHDRAWS goodwill rather than depositing it
- "Better to say nothing at all than to automate"

### Proactive Price DECREASE on Existing Customers Increases LTV
- Portfolio data: second highest LTV segment was at a FAR LOWER price
- Ultra-low churn because they only paid for what they used
- Proactively reaching out: "You're only using 4 of 6 features. Want to drop to a lower rate?"
- Those people NEVER leave

## FROM $100M LOST CHAPTERS

### Discount + One-Time Fee (Owen Parking Lot Story)
- Owen told Hormozi in a parking lot: "Charge a one-time fee alongside your discount"
- Changed everything about how Gym Launch structured front-end offers

**Four objectives of the one-time fee:**
1. **Liquidate acquisition cost**: The fee covers what you spent to get them in the door
2. **Pay salesman commission**: Commission comes out of the fee, not monthly revenue
3. **Increase buy-in of customer**: Higher upfront investment = higher commitment = lower churn
4. **Cover higher onboarding cost**: First month always costs more to deliver (setup, orientation, etc.)

**Examples:**
- 95% off first month + $1,900 setup fee
- 88% off first month + $1,000 setup fee
- $5K setup fee + $267/month ongoing
- The discount attracts attention, the fee captures cash

**4-Step Creation Process:**
1. **Pick fee name**: Setup fee, initiation fee, onboarding fee, activation fee, enrollment fee -- whatever sounds natural for your industry
2. **Pick fee price**: Should be 3-5x your monthly rate minimum. Higher = more liquidation.
3. **Pick reason why**: "Covers your personalized onboarding," "Includes your starter kit," "Funds your dedicated account setup"
4. **Start charging/discounting/waiving**: Use the fee as a lever -- charge it standard, discount it for urgency, waive it for commitment

### Initiation Fee Expanded Mechanics
- The initiation fee is pure gross profit (costs nothing incremental to collect)
- Even if waived as a promotion, its existence creates perceived value
- Can be paired with any discount strategy:
  - "First month 50% off + waived initiation fee" (double incentive)
  - "No initiation fee if you commit to 6 months" (trades fee for commitment)
  - "Initiation fee reduced from $500 to $200 this week" (partial waiver = still captures cash)
- Track: what % of customers would have bought WITHOUT the fee waiver? That's your real cost.

### Collect up front on big tickets (vid: uaLNfijnp-8 — "Scale or Fail: Hussle, dentistry")

Stop billing per visit. Offer card or financing, with a payment plan as the downsell, and say it's normal since they'll pay either way and you pre-order for their case. Hussle called this his biggest unlock in monthly revenue.

### Rebook on the debrief call (vid: mRlSb0O5QNU — "Scale or Fail: Joey, event planning")

Within two days of an event: debrief call, next year's date. Send a VSL first covering the post-event questions (billing, logistics, next event). More prep makes a better event, so booking now is genuinely better for them. Prepaying also shifts cash out of tax season.

### Pull cash forward by making payers go first (vid: MPHn-nW30QU — "14 Years of Good Advice")

Film lab asked how to bring cash forward: call and ask for payment before the work, and if they refuse, they go to the back of the line. Those who pay get theirs first.
Repeat customers who aren't on subscription: blanket purchase agreements or multiples prepaid. Credit terms are a supply and demand matter, so raise demand and you can change them. Everyone in the industry extends credit and is broke, which is a reason to question it.
Fast cash when you need it: existing customers prepay 2-3 months for a small discount; past customers get prior spend as credit toward a bigger package; your list gets a giveaway or buy-X-get-Y, or five prepaid one-on-one spots.

### Pay-per-show churns on surprise bills and blamed closes (vid: I0nCuqu7Cac — "Hotline: mental toughness and offers")

Remodeling lead agency charging $500 to $600 per show at a cost of about $210 to $250. Hormozi and Leila ran this model and name two failure points.
1. Clients retain when they can predict the bill. Send a running tab weekly or at every $1,000, and ask their budget at onboarding. One surprise invoice looks bad; by the second they're gone.
2. Clients ask why they pay for shows that didn't close, which tempts you into doing their selling. Don't. Move up market to buyers who run sales teams and already accept that a show isn't a sale.

### Don't cut churn, drag members to month six (vid: lidFtb1Ga0o — "He Makes $1.5M and Keeps 1.3%")

Hormozi's reframe: he claims churn past month six runs about 2% a month across memberships, and blended numbers hide that few cohorts ever get there. So ask how to get them to six months, not how to halve churn. Three drop points to curate: day 30, day 90, month six.
Find the activation event and sort for it: who they are, and what they did. At Gym Launch, a first high-ticket close inside seven days flipped the owner's belief; average cash collected in the first 30 days was $30K early on and about $15K to $16K later as CAC rose. For a DM funnel seller the activation is a record cash month, so require prior phone sales experience up front.

### Prepay take rates and the payment ask ladder (vid: 2nTkffKVnas — "12 rules of thumb, trades, learning")

Pay 10 months, get 2 free: expect 15-20% to take it. Moderate discount plus a kicker (skip the line, dedicated concierge, doubled guarantee): expect 30-40%. A financing provider he knows quotes about 35% more total sales; financing will not rescue a bad product.
Ask order: sell a duration, offer prepay with kickers, then half now and half in 30 days, then three payments, even on a 6 or 12 month term. Payments need not track delivery. Align dates to payday or to when their deposits land.

### Project business with no retention? Chunk up one level (vid: TLpLmhWqKEU — "Key man risk and caller breakdowns")

Architecture firm question: you will not retain at the building level, so retain at the level above it. Example: Allan software failed selling lead nurture to small businesses with inconsistent leads, so it moved to agencies, which already had leads.
An agency with 10 chiropractors at $2,000 a month may lose two and gain two each month, yet keeps using the software. Logos churned underneath while revenue retention at the agency level was strong.
For project work, your nodes are the contractors and referral partners. Sell to them, know what each is worth and costs to acquire, and measure retention there.

### Fix what you are bad at by interviewing your top 0.2% (vid: 9OsUfwTTslc — "Goal setting 1/0 live Q&A")

Hormozi admits his own gyms had only about average churn. At Gym Launch he asked who had kept churn very low for 6 months, and interviewed that top 0.2%. Each had about 100 things they did, but all of them shared five.
He standardized the five, gave them to a small test group, churn improved a lot, then repeated with 10 more gyms. Only then did he teach it, openly saying he was no expert, here is what he found and what it did for the test gyms.
Steal for any consulting or B2B offer: don't fake the weak spot, source the answer from your own customers and prove it before you claim it.

### Fix SaaS churn: live onboarding and billing to the value cycle (vid: _ooj0V4hpgw — "Hormozi Hotline caller breakdowns")

AI SaaS for real estate agents, $3.3M, wants $10M. $60 a month or $360 a year; 50% make a first video in week one, 10% edit a photo. Drop the 'not scalable' objection for now: two group Zoom onboardings a day, booked in the signup flow, 20 minutes, walking everyone through both activation actions. Watching them will rewrite the checklist.
Segment by channel, avatar (houses sold) and action. If low-volume agents churn on quiet months, align billing: quarterly or annual. Test removing monthly for a week, yet keep monthly long-term as the fast feedback loop. Cut churn by two-thirds and the business triples, so don't scale before that.

### School's churn map: months 1, 3 and 6 (extends drag members to month six) (vid: 3fsJFUvA6Ts — "What Makes The Perfect Business")

Hormozi citing School's data across hundreds of thousands of memberships: over 20% churn in month one, about 10% more at month three, a last drop at month six, then near 2% a month in every category. So spend effort in order: first 30 days, a reason to pass month three, then walk them to six.

### Billing cadence vs churn: the ProfitWell numbers (vid: 41EvCgwPrDc — "Make More Profit than 99% of People")

Hormozi cites a ProfitWell study across memberships: monthly billing churns about 10.7% a month, quarterly about 5%, annual about 2% (all stated monthly). Back of napkin LTV = price / churn, so $100 a month is worth about $935 on monthly and $5,000 on annual, roughly 5x from cadence alone. Caveat he raises: people who can prepay are also better customers, so not all of it is causal.

### The lookback window (vid: 41EvCgwPrDc — "Make More Profit than 99% of People")

Customers judge you on value received between one bill and the next. Agency delivers 10x then 1x: billed monthly, the client sees only the bad month and cancels. Billed yearly, they weigh all the months together and it's an easy renewal. Churn tracks billing frequency for this reason, and it matters most when results are volatile (ads, organic). Stretch the window as far as your avatar can pay.

### Sell annual at the price they'd still pay in the worst month (vid: 41EvCgwPrDc — "Make More Profit than 99% of People")

His reversal of internet marketing advice: he no longer sells for the most he can get. He'd rather take $5K for a year than pitch $12K on $1K a month if close rates hold, since many on monthly cancel around month four or five. At 2% churn about 80 of 100 are still there after a year, and each year's sales stack on the last. Annual also front-loads cash: half the conversions at 10x the upfront is still 5x cash to spend on acquisition.

### Stack VIP status on the annual option (vid: 41EvCgwPrDc — "Make More Profit than 99% of People")

Typical setup: monthly plus annual at about 16% off (pay 10, get 2 free). To push annual further, take a bad thing away (the price) and add good things: call it VIP, give two or three status perks like no queue, preferred times or members-only sessions. Naming the tier pulls people even at the same price, and they're still worth multiples over lifetime.

### Show cumulative value, but expect only a few points (vid: 41EvCgwPrDc — "Make More Profit than 99% of People")

You can stretch the lookback past the billing cycle by reporting running totals (time saved, money made, pounds lost), as Instacart does with hours saved. His honest estimate is a small lift: most people still judge last month, so it doesn't replace longer billing.

### Gym Launch priced at the average, not the worst month (vid: 5MjjpB8SPMo — "Make Everyone Want to Buy Your Stuff")

He charged about $4K a month as the most expensive in the space, and on average gyms did gain about $120K a year in profit. They still cancelled after a bad month while competitors around $800 kept theirs. His regret: 6,000 gyms at $1,000 a month would have been $6M monthly. Pain-point willingness and post-steak willingness differ. Plot units against price and LTV; there may be a sweet spot around $700.

### Monthly retention scoreboard from School (vid: -j8_YCWZ05Q — "Customers Stay FOREVER")

Track joins and cancels each month and label yourself growing, flat or declining: sell 5 and lose 4 is growth, sell 5 and lose 5 is flat. Hormozi's School averages for online or consumer services (not insurance or alarms): 80% monthly retention is typical, 90% is best in class, under 70% means fix retention before anything else. Going from 20% to 10% churn doubles LTV, since small churn numbers swing big.

### What each churn checkpoint needs (extends drag members to month six) (vid: -j8_YCWZ05Q — "Customers Stay FOREVER")

Month one: activation, onboarding, expectations, and selling the right promise. Day 90: a real outcome or change in behavior. Month six: connections with other people inside. Do not overhaul the business because month one is the worst; it always is. A membership under 90 days or six months old will show inflated churn since nobody has aged past the drop points yet.

### Two interviews: why you left, why you stayed (vid: -j8_YCWZ05Q — "Customers Stay FOREVER")

DM cancellers, tag each reason in a sheet (price, overwhelm, not using, missing feature, not expected), and every 20 or so cancels fix the top one or two; never react to one reply. Then ask the longest-tenured members what made them stay and what they value most. Whatever repeats is your retention engine, and everything else is optional. School's founder Sam sits next to his head of support to keep that loop tight.

### Price complaints: tier for ideal customers, ignore the rest (vid: -j8_YCWZ05Q — "Customers Stay FOREVER")

If your ideal customers say too expensive, add a cheaper plan and keep the high tier. If non-ideal people say it, price is doing its job as a filter. School added a $9 tier beside $99, holding back admin and URL extras, so hobbyists could stay until they made money.

### Overwhelm math: paying for five, using one (extends School Games simplification) (vid: -j8_YCWZ05Q — "Customers Stay FOREVER")

Using one of five things feels like getting 20% of the value; the same price for just that one feels like 100%. Planet Fitness surveyed members, found most used a sliver of a full club, and sold only that for far less. Hormozi says one School community deleted content and went from 30% to 5% monthly churn, about 6x LTV. Survey: if I deleted everything, what one thing would you fight for? If I removed one thing, what would you not miss?

### Design for both extremes and move the ladder up front (vid: -j8_YCWZ05Q — "Customers Stay FOREVER")

Busy members get a start-here path and a weekly cadence so they never feel behind; heavy users get optional side quests. Snakes and ladders: remove snakes (friction, confusion, overwhelm), and put the longest ladder, whatever makes people stick, into onboarding so they hit a fast win first. Default cadence if unsure: one big thing a week plus a best-of post.

### People churn from memberships, not payment plans (vid: 1UhvBSQFy6A — "10x Revenue with 1 New Sales Process")

Hormozi's friend could not hold a $300 monthly membership past about three months. Same product sold as a $10,000 price on a generous payment plan: average stay about eight months. An agency friend did similar. Convert: sell the big thing, spread payments, then auto-renew at the same payment when the plan ends. Name changes the odds; the business just sees regular payments.

### A stick problem is usually an avatar problem (vid: BYpTRiRqS1Y — "4 Educational Business Owners")

Caller's clients don't activate. Split winners into demographics, quantifiables (like business size) and behaviors, compared to lookalikes who failed. Sell only that avatar and turn the winning behaviors into the activation process. CAC rises but stick gets fixed. Gym Launch would have died selling all fitness people; trainers with 10 clients couldn't pay and were a separate DIY business.

### Back-end downsell when the information is used up (vid: MwhsM7qijy8 — "Increase LTV Without More Customers")

Information loses value right after it is learned, which drives churn in coaching and e-learning. When a member says they got what they need, ask what they would like to keep. Community often stays worth paying for, as does what can be consumed again: troubleshooting and accountability, since you need holding to account again next month.

### Experience outlasts results (vid: TooAB8Ow6cQ — "3 Hours of Money Making Advice")

Amazing results with a bad experience: they leave as soon as someone else can get them the result. Amazing experience with mediocre results: they stay because they love you. To convert, sell the outcome (come for the bikini); to keep them, make the experience the point (stay for the community). People find it hard to put a value on community up front, so don't lead with it.

### Guru model vs web of ties (vid: TooAB8Ow6cQ — "3 Hours of Money Making Advice")

The usual setup ties each customer only to you: one break and they leave, and everyone wanting you wears you out. Better: each customer has ties to several others, so one break costs nothing. Moving over doesn't mean showing up less; keep what you do and add ties between members. Map pins, intro posts and photos exist so people can find what they share. One person can't reward each member often enough to make them stick, but a culture where members reward one another can.

### Refunds as protection, and the friendly goodbye (vid: TooAB8Ow6cQ — "3 Hours of Money Making Advice")

Extends make it easy to cancel. Sam Ovens's account of who actually gets in trouble: no refunds, disputes climb, payment processor drops them, enough complaints reach the attorney general, then an investigation finds everything. A Stripe ban is the canary. Give the money back fast: don't become someone's project, since that $6K matters more to them, and many return. Hormozi's Planet Fitness case: a location needs about 10,000 members and can get them in a market of 50,000, despite about 5% monthly churn, because a pleasant exit means people sign up again when the next job comes or they feel fat. It cycles through the town.

### SaaS retention rebuild: three problem buckets, $2M saved (vid: afbP6sB_Atc — "Keep more customers")

Portfolio SaaS, 55+ non-technical buyers, churn spiking 90 days after launch. Fixes: onboarding, expectations, incentives. Reported in six months: about $2M saved, 61% fewer cancellations, one in four people on an escalation call bought a higher tier, and company-wide ascensions rose from 20% to 48%. Extra upsell revenue is on top of that and drops to the bottom line since CAC is already paid.

### Onboarding pilot: from webinar to one-on-one (vid: afbP6sB_Atc — "Keep more customers")

Before: 25 buyers per call, company story, a third of the time on password resets, a hard pitch, then nothing. Pilot on 15% of new customers, with the required stats decided in advance. One-on-one using CLOSER's reinforce step: restate why they came and the goal. Quick win: logged in and shown around, support pulled in live. End with notes (so they needn't take any), tutorials for the features they named, and a live intro to one point of contact. At ALAN, tying next steps to the buyer's stated why raised follow-through by 2x to 3x as he recalls it. Better service cut the need to push: the next offer sold more with less pressure.

### Complaints are the value equation inverted (vid: afbP6sB_Atc — "Keep more customers")

Too techy means effort. Not enough support means lower perceived likelihood. Taking too long means time delay. Before adding product, listen to sales calls for how the promise got set. Rescripting to lower expectations left close rates unchanged, since an over-promise can breed doubt, while a realistic one is believed. Perceived support was an access problem: support existed, so they put a chat bubble in the software. Bonus: which page people clicked for help became the product road map.

### Customer rescue runs the reverse of lead nurture (vid: afbP6sB_Atc — "Keep more customers")

A business is a bow tie. Leads get heavy outreach that tapers; an at-risk customer gets the opposite: one attempt today, two tomorrow, three the next, across call, text and email. Treat it as you would a relative in trouble. Script CS like sales, since 90% raise the same few issues, and practice it. Unhappy customers pick up, and letting them vent often sells: one in four buying something 4x to 5x the price covers the other three.

### The proactive downgrade (vid: afbP6sB_Atc — "Keep more customers")

In another portfolio company, the second-highest LTV group was customers using a few of the features who were moved, unasked, to a tier at half the price. They essentially never cancel. Better a customer for much longer than one gone in two months, plus word of mouth and testimonials. Match contact to ticket: low-ticket buyers like to hear from you a lot, while affluent buyers mostly want it to just work.

### "I already got the value" is an exit line: push the work (vid: bgBIO6nZawg — "7 Millionaires")

Caller: CPA firm near $3M, about 70% annual retention, target over 80%. Hormozi doesn't buy that clients left after learning a few tricks. He'd audit the sales-to-onboarding handoff, take better notes on each client, and send frequent updates needing no reply: we ran this today, it moves forward, nothing needed from you. Clients can't judge the technical work, so they read it from the communication, and a cheaper rival loses to "these guys work for me constantly."

### Watch attendance fall before they cancel (vid: h6y0nYVZgwE — "Not Unbelievably Rich Yet")

Gym data shows a visible slide: three times a week, then two, then one, then cancel. Track it and call at the first drop, so they know they were missed; reach out every two weeks even without a reason. The first 24-48 hours after sale deserve the most effort: who greets them, when, who reconfirms. He claims two to five extra months paid from this alone.

### A refund is only an apology: expect to go negative on a mess-up (vid: p39nLmVtOjc — "You Made Your Customers ANGRY")

A refund puts cash back, not the time, hopes and hassle the customer spent, so you are still below neutral. Two poles from his own life: a gym equipment seller who shipped a bent frame, blamed the accepted delivery and wanted more money for a part (machine thrown out, seller trashed to anyone who asks); and a Ritz manager after a three-and-a-half-hour dinner, who comped it, upgraded rooms, sent gifts for all eight, booked other restaurants and showed up to seat them. Hormozi now sends everyone to that manager. When a seven-person visit to his HQ hit a scheduling error, some flying internationally, they refunded the fee and travel and added dinner and personal consulting. ROI is out the window: the aim is tragic to neutral to champion, and you never want to become an angry customer's project.

### Angry boat: be more upset than they are (vid: p39nLmVtOjc — "You Made Your Customers ANGRY")

Nobody can talk someone out of feeling wronged; only they can shrink it. Minimizing or citing the email you sent says they don't matter, so they escalate, and a one-star review is the customer playing judge when nothing else worked. So get there first: own it at once, call it unacceptable, validate. Show more upset than they feel and they'll be the one saying it's not that big a deal. That sentence has to come from them. It's always your fault as owner, since fault you own is something you can fix.

### Lawsuits follow dislike, not mistakes (vid: p39nLmVtOjc — "You Made Your Customers ANGRY")

Extends the angry boat with a fact from his physician parents: doctors who get sued for malpractice are the ones patients hate, not the ones who err most. A patient told by her lawyer the real error was by the doctor she loved dropped it. An acquaintance in hot sauce mailed a customer missing caps with a handwritten note and extras; that review flipped from one star to five, and he then put a note and spare cap in every kit.

### Give the front line a box of fixes (vid: p39nLmVtOjc — "You Made Your Customers ANGRY")

Stolen from hotels. High-transaction business? Staff who can't fix anything get blamed anyway. Pre-approve two or three remedies by severity (drink credits, a free meal, a comped night and upgrade), bake the cost into margin, and set a monthly usage cap, for example five. ### Unlockables: bonuses placed just past where people quit (vid: sPkMHh8zTMI — "7 Ways to Get People to Buy More Times")

Decide what you want customers to do, then put a reward on it: activate, testimonial, referral, buy the next tier. Or tie it to tenure alone. If people stay four months, drop the jackpot at month five. Widen the gaps with bigger rewards, say months 3, 8 and 15, since people will wait longer for larger hits. ### Retention install order by bang for buck (extends five retention strategies) (vid: sPkMHh8zTMI — "7 Ways to Get People to Buy More Times")

### Customer contact runs opposite to lead contact (vid: sPkMHh8zTMI — "7 Ways to Get People to Buy More Times")

Leads get many touches on day one, tapering off. A customer who slips gets one touch the first day, two the next, then three, escalating. Attendance falling from three to two visits is your early warning, since cancellation only lags it. A customer who knows someone noticed usually returns to full use.

### Split the weekly report into daily set-and-meet (vid: sPkMHh8zTMI — "7 Ways to Get People to Buy More Times")

Same work, more wins felt. Rather than one weekly summary from your agency, say today we work on X, then next day say X is done and tomorrow is Y. That's seven positive cycles per week instead of one. Repeat the onboarding plan often too: they are on their phone with kids in the back seat, so let them assemble it from many tellings.

### Find the retention point, then pull it forward (vid: yPDQCfrwh8E — "Simple Strategy for 60 Days")

Every product has a moment that turns a customer into a long-term one; shows have an episode after which viewers finish the series. Point all communication and resources at it. At Gym Launch, a high ticket sale in a gym's first 7 days tripled LTV, so that became the only goal, later measured by how fast they launched the internal campaign. With profit that large at stake, spend lavishly: get on a call with a tech person and send the email together before hanging up.

### Same work, more visible: 14 touches instead of one (vid: yPDQCfrwh8E — "Simple Strategy for 60 Days")

An agency that reports every two weeks reinforces the client once; one that sends what it did each day reinforces 14 times, doing the same work. Chop any outcome into small leading indicators of progress so time to first value feels shorter without changing delivery.

### Choreographed onboarding: promises and four handoff moves (vid: yPDQCfrwh8E — "Simple Strategy for 60 Days")

Extends the first 48 hours. Open and close four to six promises in the first 24 hours; split one task with six checkpoints into six kept promises. Then: 1) Sales books the first CS meeting live before hanging up, so nobody sits in no man's land. 2) Capture the goal on the sales call and have CS quote it ("Johnny told me you're doing this for the wedding"). 3) Restate exactly what sales promised, since disconnect drives early refunds, then resell with stories of people like them. 4) Lay out the steps to the target and keep engaging on them. No silver bullet: churn falls from many small fixes.
