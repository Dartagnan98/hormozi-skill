# Focus
## Source: Alex Hormozi (602 video transcripts)

## Stop Starting New Businesses
- Hormozi had 6 gyms, 2 agencies, a turnaround business -- barely making money
- DUI/head-on collision was the catalyst to consolidate
- Ended 3 partnerships, sold/gave away gyms, focused entirely on Gym Launch
- Result: went from scattered millions to $3M profit in 12 months

## Attention > Time
- "The most valuable thing we have as entrepreneurs is not our time but our attention"
- Unmade decisions accumulate and consume thinking power
- Each unmade decision allocates a portion of your brain, reducing decision-making power
- "I'm always one decision away from changing my life"

## The Trash Business Neighbor Story
- Neighbor sold trash business for a lot of money, waited out non-compete, started same business again
- "Why would I learn something new? I already know everything about the trash business"
- Most entrepreneurs desire novelty when they should desire depth

## Trade Novelty for Loyalty
- "You become loyal to the business"
- Business is like fine wine -- you understand it deeper over the years
- "You miss out on that opportunity if you're going after one night stands from a business perspective"
- Channel ADD into marketing/content: new hooks, new creative, new ad ideas
- Hormozi puts his ADD into content -- "I've become a significantly better entrepreneur by making content because it distracts me from messing up my business"

## The Decision Etymology
- "Decide" -- root "cide" means "to kill" (suicide, homicide)
- A decision is choosing to kill off all other options
- "You need to decide what your business is going to be and kill off all other options"

## The 3 Evil Es of Entrepreneurship

### Evil E #1: Expectations
- Setting arbitrary timelines on arbitrary numbers
- Leads to shortcuts, hasty high-risk decisions, destroying the business
- **Fix**: Shift from outcome-based to process-based expectations
- "Good process drives good results" (from mentor)
- Keep high expectations but put them on PROCESS, not outcome

### Evil E #2: Emotions
- Best CEOs (Bezos, Munger, Buffett) are devoid of emotion in decisions
- Red flag: flip-flopping on decisions when presented same data
- **Tactics**:
  - When you feel urgency, EXTEND the time horizon (sleep on it)
  - Make big decisions from a state of contentment and gratitude
  - "The best decisions I've made came from a state of contentment"
  - If you don't feel content/grateful, don't make the decision

### Evil E #3: Expenses
- Expenses = liabilities = risk
- "Get the growth first, then growth pays for the people"
- Don't hire in anticipation of growth -- growth will pay the expenses
- Hormozi hired 25 support reps anticipating growth for Prestige Labs, now 4 people handle more
- When you anticipate growth, culture decreases, morale decreases, efficiency decreases
- **Self-reinforcing loop**: Bad expectations (from deficiency) -> emotional decisions -> bad expenses -> worse outcomes -> worse expectations

### Most Deadly: Expectations
- "Call all your beliefs assumptions -- they become far more malleable"
- "This is my assumption, what do you think?" vs "This is my expectation, what do you think?"

## FOCUS FRAMEWORKS (Batch 7 -- Expanded)

### Bucket Analogy for Business Growth
- Business = bucket, traffic = water flowing in
- Step 1: Fix holes in bucket (better: improve what exists)
- Step 2: Increase flow to bucket (more: scale what works)
- Step 3: Add new flows (new: only after exhausting better and more)
- Most entrepreneurs: multiple leaky buckets, no bandwidth to fix any of them
- Diverting resources from core bucket to build new buckets = both buckets deteriorate
- "Media costs go up, talent costs go up. A business that isn't improving is getting worse relative to the market over time"

### "Better, More, New" Priority Framework (Expanded)
- Always ask: Have we maxed out BETTER yet?
- Better = lowest risk opportunity everyone has
- More = riskier than better (scaling what works)
- New = riskiest (innovation, new products)
- Only move to More after Better is exhausted, only New after More

### Cost of Distraction (Half-Built Bridges)
- "Most people learn two, three, four of the skills but there's ten they need to learn, and they say 'this isn't for me' and jump to another one"
- "It is arrogant to believe that you are going to out-compete people who are fully focused on one thing when you're split on five"
- First lesson to unlearn: innovation was reinforced early. "The Innovation already happened when you got product-market fit. As soon as people started buying, NEW needs to end for a while"
- "Mark Zuckerberg didn't have side hustles"

## FOCUS EXPANSION (Deep Pass -- Batches 3-4)

### Do Nothing New = Best Results
- Company events: rule of "zero new things, just execute known good things"
- Four times in a row: best NPS scores ever
- "Every time we change things, NPS goes down"

### Busy But Broke = Too Many Things
- Any change creates **guaranteed 20% decrease** before potential upside
- Need at least 20% upside to justify guaranteed 20% downside
- In 18 months, grabbed only **3 items** from his 200+ idea list
- "The biggest problem in your business is lack of execution, not lack of new ideas"
- "You will feel so much relief when you take your to-do list and say 'this is no longer my to-do list'"

### One Priority Framework
- "Priority" from Latin meaning "first" -- you can only have ONE first
- Test: "If 100% certain you could accomplish ONE thing in a year, which gets you furthest?"
- "People do things faster linearly than in parallel"
- Warren Buffett's punch card: 20 investment decisions for life = much better decisions

### Shorts Editor Story
- One editor split across two platforms -- both suffered
- Focused on one platform: **got MORE views** than both combined when split

### The Stuck Lady at HQ
- Kept innovating sales process (the thing she loved)
- Sales wasn't the constraint -- she was already good at it
- Marketing/lead gen was the constraint, had it outsourced and it showed
- "Some stuff is good enough because it's not the constraint"

## FOCUS ADDITIONS (Batch 5-6)

### The Magnifying Glass Metaphor
- Your potential/intelligence/work ethic = size of magnifying glass (how much sun it captures)
- Doesn't matter how big it is if it's not focused on one point
- "The more talented you are, the more opportunities you know you could crush, which just means more things you have to say no to"

### Only 2-3 Entrepreneurial Seasons Left
- "I had a big realization... deep sadness where I realized I could only do a few things throughout the rest of my life"
- Plans acquisition.com to be his final swing: "Do this until I get too old to do it"

### The Dress Test for Business Ideas
- See dress in store -> decide not to buy -> if still thinking about it a month later, go back and buy it
- Same for business ideas: write it down, sleep on it, if still compelling after 2-4 weeks, it might have legs

### Known hard beats unknown hard (vid: 138y93ywE_g — "3 signs of a successful entrepreneur")

Before a second business or revenue line, ask: does anything logically stop the current business from doing 2x, 5x, or 10x besides it being hard? The new thing only looks easier because you haven't done it yet. It will also be hard. Take the known hard.

### Coats of paint on one problem (vid: 138y93ywE_g — "3 signs of a successful entrepreneur")

Surface area of thinking: how many passes have you made over this problem, in different moods, after different conversations, compared against different things? Diverse lenses over time produce the complete, elegant answer. Only repetition on one narrow problem gets you there, which is why the unfocused lose.

### Distraction is intellectual laziness (vid: fj5uxdv_j5Y — "97% of how to get rich")

Most owners know on some level what needs to happen. Because they don't know how to solve it, they go solve something they can, which feels competent and changes nothing. If you're distracted, ask what task you're avoiding because you'd have to be incompetent at it for a while.

### Do more of fewer (vid: mRlSb0O5QNU — "Scale or Fail: Joey, event planning")

Owners think the business is limited because they're doing too little. It's usually because they're trying too much. A lot of things can work, but none will if you try them all. Find what makes the most money and ruthlessly remove everything else.

### The focus rules flip for local businesses (vid: Uj5XKwW0Vbs — "End of life discussion")

Math tutoring company asked whether to add subjects. Online: stay on math, there's no shortage of struggling students. Local: open to expanding if teachers are as easy to find and it adds little operational drag.
Why: a local market caps demand. Once you've done everything to reach it, growth has to come from LTV and cross-sells. Same reason his one-channel-to-$1M rule holds for everyone except local.

### Reallocate to the winner, then count true fixed costs (vid: TLgSkSQUql4 — "Answering your sales questions live")

One operator stuck running two companies: a commercial locksmith with no margin, and a security side business at 45% net margins with a working acquisition channel.
His math: all the effort that fixes the weak one might yield $200K in margin. The same effort could 4x the strong one to about $1M profit. That is an $800K delta plus far fewer headaches.
Then list only truly fixed costs on the weak business: labor and software do not count, leases and truck payments might. Let the winner cover that baseline, move the A players over, and judge results at the owner level, since both LLCs roll up to one person.

### Switching businesses: write the removal plan before the start plan (vid: 0RjIw9M1j28 — "Walk Away From $1.4M Business?")

Caller: $1.4M implementation firm with a fuzzy avatar, weighing a clear-avatar healthcare business. Hormozi is fine with burning the boats, not with straddling.
Test: list everything you can strip from the old business, count the hours left in the week, and only start if those hours could really win. If not, add a direct acquisition channel to the existing business instead.
His claims: all in, the new thing grows five times faster, and your year two otherwise becomes year four. Growth speed tracks how fast the boss decides; delayed decisions eat the most time.

### Write the give-up list before the to-do list (vid: 9OsUfwTTslc — "Goal setting 1/0 live Q&A")

Plateaued for 6-12 months while working all your hours means you are doing the wrong things, and you can't add to a full plate. The bigger the goal, the bigger the list of things to cut.
Going from six gyms toward 10, Hormozi gave up football (the games, commentary, matchups, fantasy, and the friendships built on them) and Netflix for that season.
Test for anything you won't cut: say it out loud as a sentence. I did not hit my potential because of the Ravens. If that sounds pathetic, it goes.

### Anxiety is many plans and few priorities (vid: 9OsUfwTTslc — "Goal setting 1/0 live Q&A")

Hormozi's working definition: anxiety means you see lots of paths and don't know which to take. Sadness is the opposite, seeing no path at all. So the fix for anxiety is mechanical: force rank everything, and let number one beat the rest.
He then reduces each goal to input and output, and calls strategy nothing more than priorities: limited time and money against unlimited options. Many owners make money by accident and spray across channels without knowing 90% of sales come from one; 10x that one and forget the others.

### Count workflows, not revenue streams (vid: ehmBlLNGgsA — "Focus is subtraction live Q&A")

Asked by a biotech owner with 75% of income from one stream whether to cut the rest. His test is what the extra stream changes about the team's day. If behavior barely changes and you get more from the same work, keep it. If it needs a new cycle of actions, new talent, or harder-to-find customers, double down on the main stream instead.
The line he attacks: you get rich first, then diversify. That is the order millionaires with seven income streams actually followed.

### Too many good opportunities means your goal is too small (vid: ehmBlLNGgsA — "Focus is subtraction live Q&A")

For someone who sees value everywhere and says yes too often. Poor, almost anything beats poor. At $1M a year, plenty of things still clear the bar. Set the goal at $100M a year and the qualifying options shrink to maybe one. Raise the goal instead of building a filter.

### Run a timer on your real work (vid: 7IPgbO7-O2M — "Birthday stream on priorities")

His crazy-sounding number: 4 to 6 hours of truly focused work daily, seven days a week (28 hours at the low end), with a timer you restart whenever you look away. That alone puts you ahead of most people.
Lesson from remodeling his own gym floor by hand: unlike a clock-in, the floor only moved when he did the work, and progress was visible daily. Many show up expecting credit for being present.

### Put a price tag on the wall (vid: 07jC6ooRIHw — "Sick of Shiny Object Syndrome")

The real constraint shows up at scale as a concrete wall of unknown thickness, and people flee it for a new idea. Frame the cost beside the prize: he told a founder rebuilding a media company into software that it was very hard and worth an extra $250M, and the work suddenly felt reasonable. Much of his job is expectation setting: if $100M in value takes five years, are you actually willing to wait five years?

### Stop dating your business (vid: 07jC6ooRIHw — "Sick of Shiny Object Syndrome")

Marriage gave Hormozi back an estimated 30% to 40% of the attention he spent on possibilities. Founders do the same with side deals, keeping other ventures warm, and partnership lunches. Commit and that attention returns to the main thing. Also: the better you get at business, the more attractive the shiny objects become, so saying no to bad ones proves nothing.

### Conquer the city before the country (vid: BYpTRiRqS1Y — "4 Educational Business Owners")

Home flipper with a local coaching and contracting ecosystem, about $4M, wants a national stage. Hormozi: armies that overexpand collapse. He's at a fraction of local capacity (8x more sales fit the current setup), so fortify the base first with ads and sales. The "impact" itch needs no restructure: make free content on what he already does and let the new COO free up the time.

### Best diet, best spouse, best business (vid: F84olnKkseM — "Business Was Hard, 4 Concepts")

The best one is whichever you stick with, since commitment makes it the best. He scores focus by giving himself a mental point for each no. Beware the fallacy of success: that a switch worked doesn't prove it was right. Had he stayed with gyms, he thinks he'd have 200 to 300 locations, worth about what he has now. Hard to imagine someone doing one business for 50 years, fixing each mistake, and failing. Test for "owner": if a problem makes your phone ring, the business owns you.

### Every scaled business ends in the same day (vid: F84olnKkseM — "Business Was Hard, 4 Concepts")

At the top, any business means managing a handful of smart heads of marketing, sales, finance, legal and ops. If you hate the current grind, switching just reaches the same end state in a new industry, so beat this boss. Staying in love with it: entrepreneurs fade, businesses don't die. Turn "the market doesn't like us" into "I don't know how to market this" and it becomes a solvable skill gap. Every six months the problems change, so it feels like a new business.

### Organic sellers keep adding products when audience is the constraint (vid: 8C_6qojTA78 — "Helping E-Commerce Owners Scale")

Extends Red Dress. Caller with a salon (about $900K at 50%) and hair extensions ($2.6M at 30%) wanted to self-fund a $500K SaaS for the same buyers. His pattern: an audience buys product one, so you make product two, and in 2-3 years four mini businesses share one pool when you needed a bigger pool. A customer list does not sell everything. Software means committing to no profit for about seven years against rivals with no side businesses. Money already spent is sunk: some of the best money ever spent is deciding to stop. To stay honest he asked first: argue from your position, or tell you what I think?

### Winning = doing the work your informed future self calls perfect (vid: Ktwv_uEW-uA — "What now?")

Fear works best in the short term, reward over the long haul, so a marathon needs a redefined win. His: imagine a future you with full context on your skills and resources, who knows what undistracted maximum effort on this looks like. Hitting that is the win. The catch he accepts: that standard only fits a few projects.

### Four filters for opportunities that can reach billions (vid: LMlbWtUFa4E — "How Billionaires Make Their Billions")

Do not pursue something capped at $100M a year. Ask: not limited by your time, serves a very big market, very high gross margin, and can be run on a very long horizon. Aim for $10B or $50B, not $1B.

### Productivity = work per unit of time x leverage (vid: PFKGHL1MqkU — "Investor 3min Morning Routine")

Define the word and most routines fail: adding non-work cannot raise work per hour. A guy whose coach had him on walks, red light and cold plunge until 11am would do better prospecting those hours. Invert it: the worst routine is long, has many steps that burn mental energy, and you cannot work without it. If you say it warms you up, test that: remove one step at a time. Then order the day by leverage, highest first.

### Thinking of teaching your industry? Don't straddle (vid: Rm4zRdLAyjw — "Easiest Million Dollar Business")

Three owners asked him about it: a $4M painter, a six-location lash salon at $3M to $4M, a backyard garden installer. They know enough to teach beginners but not to master anything, and the income jump looks great right before their real business pays off. The impact high wears off: he went numb on gym transformations after hundreds of testimonials, and clients' wins will go the same way. Becker (to Hyros) and Ovens (to Skool) moved after $25M to $30M a year, per his figures, and dropped the old thing. A leftover business at 10% of income can eat 50 to 100% of your attention when something goes wrong.

### Three gates before quitting the job (vid: SYkwtqFoRcM — "Go ALL IN on your side hustle")

Extends "no side hustles": his definition is a main hustle not yet big enough to support you; side money for its own sake is a distraction. Gates, in order of risk: live under your salary and bank about six months of expenses; get the new thing to match your income; hold that level for six months, since one good month then a drop will wreck your selling. Exception by rate: 4 hours a week earning half your salary can reasonably project to a full match. He saved $50K over two years on a $50K salary before leaving.

### Rotating between three games costs more than a third (vid: SasEJE4FI-I — "Your Failures Can Make You Rich")

Extends the woman in the red dress. Alternate A, B, C and you lose time plus the rust of getting back up to speed each switch, so the rival playing only one isn't 3x ahead, in his estimate 6x to 9x. Test: how would you set up if you had to win this game with no option of failing? No other games, and your whole life oriented around this one. Jobs's check, per Hormozi: focus means refusing ideas you desperately want, not just the bad ones.

### Change has a guaranteed price and no guaranteed payoff (vid: ULGT0Qpglek — "17 Life-Changing Conversations")

From a mentor he describes as a $10B CEO: go slow to go fast. Erratic new things look fast up close but stay slow over the long run because you keep restarting at zero and never reach compounding. Treat every change as a fixed cost you will certainly pay, against a benefit that may not arrive.

### Every task must ladder to one of four levers (vid: TooAB8Ow6cQ — "3 Hours of Money Making Advice")

Traffic, conversion, price, churn. If a task doesn't map to one, why do it? The test case: reviving a dead 14,000-member free group is a traffic bet, so it must beat just spending more on ads or making better ones; Sam's question was whether anything would break if the group didn't exist. Applying more, better, new: argue for why the new thing beats more of what works. Order for a solo operator: promotion first, because that's lifeblood, with enough flow to learn what people want; then delete and simplify; then return and scale until something breaks. Your notes from any event become about three actions, and one of them will do most of the work.

### Most things don't matter: what the PE partner listened for (vid: WsYgWC7NmO8 — "Top 10 Fast Business Lessons")

Watching the managing partner who bought two of his companies in a quarterly meeting, Hormozi saw him engage on only two topics: building in tech for a higher multiple, and accretive M&A. Those were the only paths he saw to a $250M+ sale. Everything else was blocking and tackling. Focus on the few 10x items and you can get plenty of others wrong.

### Find the shower door in your business (vid: jzKpAtzKQ54 — "You NEED to Know This Number")

A partner left a residential glass repair chain and sold only shower doors: lots of buyers, cheap to deliver, one installer doing 5 to 10 a day where custom work could take months. Lower ticket, better LTV to CAC, repeatable. Same read for a pool designer selling commercial, resort, residential and modular: modular had six or seven variables, good margin, a short gap from yes to installed, and more buyers.
Test: higher ratio, bigger market, faster loop, productizable. On "I'm not passionate about shower doors": at the top every business is the same job, running heads of sales, marketing and finance, so pick the one most likely to get there.

### Four hours a day on the constraint, bought for $1,500 a month (vid: h6y0nYVZgwE — "Not Unbelievably Rich Yet")

Sticky note rule for owners under about $150K a month: the first four hours, before anyone arrives (or last, if a night person), go to the single biggest problem until it's solved. A month later that's all that moved; nobody remembers the fires. Finding the hours: he estimates (stats from memory) about 96 hours a month on food, cleaning and laundry. Pay roughly $1,500 for all of it, which pays if you earn over about $15 an hour working. You'd pay that for an employee clone; you are the best one. It's a one-time gain, so use it.

### If only one thing were true, we'd win (vid: m5ordaa7NN4 — "This Idea Will Make Your Business Unstoppable")

Spend a day or a week on it: if we had more referrals than anyone, or the best paid ads, or the most traffic, we win no matter what. Name the single point of leverage, then attack it with an onslaught of hours, reps and prep for those reps. You cannot do 100x the work on 100 things.

### What are we most afraid of doing? and the 17-company question (vid: oK2_u-uS_Bo — "Money Lessons I Learned In Marriage")

Leila's strategy question, with a follow-up: what would we do if we couldn't fail? It's usually the hard conversation, so what took him six months to confront now takes a week. Focus math: running two $1M companies could be one $10M company. A friend at $10M a year ran about 17 businesses and needed a spreadsheet to count them. Asked if he could double the best one if it were his only job, he said easily, then spent a year exiting the rest and that one grew. Own many, operate one.

### The juicy no test (vid: rhVxX5_8xUw — "Business Owners, We Have a Problem")

From an interview with a Jobs executive, as Hormozi recalls it: meetings opened with "what have you said no to lately?" Obvious nos did not count, since pursuing those would be stupid anyway. It had to be something you badly wanted. Apply it to yourself: name the juicy no on your table right now. A list of easy nos means you have not focused yet.

### Don't sacrifice your first billion for your second (vid: rhVxX5_8xUw — "Business Owners, We Have a Problem")

Scale the number to your size: people chase a second $1M or $10M before finishing the first. His own record: the day he started Prestige, Gym Launch stopped growing; adding Allan as a third CEO job shrank both others; after selling Allan, Gym Launch climbed again. He turned down a billion-plus offer because it lowered the odds on Acquisition.com, the bet with fewer beliefs left to prove.

### No is a deposit, yes is a withdrawal (vid: rhVxX5_8xUw — "Business Owners, We Have a Problem")

Yes feels good today and hurts when the commitment comes due. No hurts today, since you let someone down, and pays later when you are not stuck going. His shorthand when tempted: you just want a cookie right now. Wandering eyes start when the business stops rewarding you, which is usually the boss you need to beat, not a sign to leave.

### Picture the competitor you would fear (vid: rhVxX5_8xUw — "Business Owners, We Have a Problem")

Smart, hardworking, learns from every mistake, never stops, says no to everything else, patient, in no rush. Doing two things means believing you are so much better than this person that half of you beats all of them. That is delusion. The upside: anyone can become this competitor, because the deciding trait is a choice.

### Problems vs missed opportunities: two lists (vid: zNiXk_3C_Io — "REAL Reason Not Making Money")

A problem threatens the business staying alive: complaints, a processor red flag, falling close rate. A missed opportunity is something you could add: a channel, a product, retargeting. Founders treat both as to-dos, and chase the fun ones in their favorite function. Opportunities grow problems along with the business; solved problems also grow it, and that growth comes from getting better rather than doing more. Move opportunities to a big money-making idea list, solve problems first, because they are the surest swings, then pick from the list. Most ideas look dumb two months later; after years working with Leila he reckons only about one in ten of his ideas works. Every stale idea you'd have pushed on the team was a mistake avoided.
