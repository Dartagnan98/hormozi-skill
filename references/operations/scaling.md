# Scaling
## Source: Alex Hormozi (602 video transcripts)

## The Pi Equation (Most Used Equation in Business)
- **Core formula**: Inflow / Outflow = Hypothetical Max
- Three applications:

### Application 1: Hypothetical Max Customers
- Inflow (new customers/month) / Churn rate = Max customers
- Example: 5 new customers/month / 10% churn = 50 max customers
- If currently at 30 with these rates, you're growing. If at 60, you're shrinking.

### Application 2: Lifetime Value (LTV)
- Average revenue per customer per month / Churn rate = LTV
- Example: $1,000/month / 20% churn = $5,000 LTV
- Gives you the number to market with (spend at least 1/3 of LTV to acquire)

### Application 3: Revenue Max
- New business $/month / Revenue churn rate = Revenue max
- Example: $10,000 new business/month / 10% churn = $100,000/month max

## The Golden Ratio (Net Negative Churn)
- Referral percentage > Churn percentage = infinite growth
- If 10% of customers refer monthly and 5% leave, business grows without paid advertising
- "This is the promised land"
- Product so good that organic growth exceeds churn

## Growth Curve Reality
- Growth follows a logarithmic curve approaching hypothetical max
- Rate of growth slows over time as churn eats bigger percentage
- Realistically cap at 80-90% of hypothetical max due to business volatility

## Inflow Is Your Bottleneck Framework

### The Math of Scaling (gym example)
- Want 300 members, 10% churn = need 30 new/month just to break even
- Converting 50% of trials = need 60 front-end signups/month
- 50% show rate = need 120 scheduled appointments/month
- That's 30/week, 5/day on 6-day selling schedule
- To GROW an extra 30: double everything above

### Acceptable Metrics
- Services = easy in, easy out (that's the territory)
- Churn below 1-2% is unlikely in service businesses
- "If you want to scale, your inflow never stops"
- Must sign up people AND fulfill concurrently

### Operational Preparation
- Block time: 2 hours/day for outbound (retention), 2 hours for onboarding, sales calls throughout
- Calculate cost: 15 leads/day at $10/lead = $150/day = $4,500/month
- "A lot of people don't confront the math"

### Emotional Readiness
- "Champions have short memories -- they bounce back fast"
- If you close 50%, half your meetings WILL say no
- "The most experienced sales people don't get bothered"

## The 20% Decrease Rule (Cost of Change)

### The Rule
- Every change in the business causes approximately a 20% decrease in performance
- Even if the change is superior, there's an immediate performance dip
- Team has muscle memory -- rebuilding takes time

### The Math
- If improvement only yields 5%, you took a guaranteed 20% loss for potential 5% gain
- Takes 4-5 months to make up the deficit
- Meanwhile, leaving people alone, they naturally improve ~5% on their own
- Net result: change cost you months for zero net gain

### The Decision Filter
- Only make changes with risk-adjusted 20%+ improvement
- Specifically: need 40% estimated improvement with 50/50 odds = 20% expected value
- "If I don't see a risk-adjusted 20% improvement, I make no change at all"

### Practical Application
- Keep "Alex's Big List of Ideas" on phone
- When you think of something, write it down instead of messaging the team
- If still thinking about it 2-4 weeks later, it has legs
- Team checks: "You have four other 50% return moves. Is this better than those?"
- "I have grown to accept that some shit stays shit"
- Most entrepreneurs change too many things too often -- never know what actually broke

## Punch Card Mentality (from Buffett)
- You have ~4 changes per quarter per department
- "If everybody had a punch card with 20 investments their whole life, people would do exceptionally well"
- Forces you to really think: "Is this the bet I'm going to make?"

## Relative vs Absolute Returns
- Spending more on ads: relative ROI goes down but absolute profit goes up
- $10K spend -> $100K return (10x, $90K profit) vs $1M spend -> $2M return (2x, $1M profit)
- Obviously take the $1M profit even at lower relative return

## Supply-Constrained Business Solution
1. Raise price (reduces demand, increases cash flow)
2. Use extra cash to advertise higher compensation
3. Attract talent needed to remove supply constraint
- "Usually there's two or three order consequences"

## SUPPLY-CONSTRAINED BUSINESS PLAYBOOK (Deep Pass -- Batch 3)

When you can't find enough producers/practitioners:
1. Reframe: you're in the RECRUITING business, not the service business
2. Create compelling career paths for the role
3. Use all four channels for recruiting: warm outreach, cold outreach, paid ads, organic content
4. Add headhunters/recruiters
5. Staff referral program with MASSIVE incentives ($20K-$100K per hire if they generate $350K/year)
6. As you get bigger, referral compounding becomes material

### Physical Therapy Example
- Each PT generates $350K gross profit/year
- "Why would you not pay $50K or $100K for a referral if you know they generate $350K?"
- Buddy M cleaning business: same reframe -- you're in the recruiting maids business

## ONBOARDING PLAYBOOK FOR LOW CONSUMPTION (Deep Pass -- Batch 3)

1. Closer books the onboarding call (BAMFAM -- book a meeting from a meeting)
2. 4-6 one-on-one sessions before group (adjust price if needed)
3. Each session ends with BAMFAM: pull up calendars, book next session
4. Automated reminder sequence + manual reach-out from session leader
5. Group sessions positioned as graduation/earned privilege
6. AB close: "Would you rather start one-on-one or in a group?"
   - If they choose group, gift them 1-2 one-on-one sessions anyway
- Disney stat: "It takes 37 magical moments to overcome one tragic moment"

## CONSTRAINT DIAGNOSIS FRAMEWORK (Deep Pass -- Batch 3)

Diagnostic sequence from quarterly meeting:
1. "Why do we expect it to be different this time?"
2. "Why weren't you able to do this?" -> lost reps, net zero
3. Turn to HR: "How many candidates per week?" -> 1 qualified/week
4. "How many interviews to get 1 qualified?" -> 5
5. "How many finals to get 1 hire?" -> 4
6. "What's churn?" -> 20%/quarter
7. Math reveals impossibility of hitting goal with current process

- "Every system will grow up to its constraint and no further"
- "What makes macro speed happen is knowing which problems to solve in what order"
- Result: $10-12M in missed annual revenue from solving the wrong problem for two quarters

## SCALING ADDITIONS (Batch 5-6)

### Nail It Then Scale It: Retention Math
- Company A: sells 100/year, keeps 100%. Year 2: 200. Year 3: 300
- Company B: sells 100/200/300 but loses 100/200/300 respectively
- Both look like 300 sales/year but B acquired 600 total vs A's 300
- "All of that added cost drops straight to the bottom line"
- "Advertising to acquire customers only becomes more expensive in time"

### Scaling Creates Lumpy Inefficiency (Expanded)
- New sales reps won't be as good as existing five
- New marketer won't be as good at content initially
- Business incurs full cost immediately but doesn't get return for months
- At 3:1 LTV:CAC with all manual processes, adding new people could push to unprofitable
- Need 12:1+ ratio buffer to absorb scaling inefficiency

### Local Business Expansion Tactics
1. Expand existing location (knock down walls, add seats, cut class times, extend hours)
2. Add more channels (Instagram + X + YouTube + Facebook + TikTok + etc.)
3. Open another location
- Most people's goals achievable within current vehicle -- too impatient

### Five Directions to Move Your Market
1. **Up market**: single gyms -> franchisers
2. **Down market**: gyms -> individual trainers (more people, less buying power)
3. **Adjacent**: gyms -> chiropractors
4. **Broader**: gyms -> health & wellness in general
5. **Narrower**: gyms -> only spin studios

### Fastest to $10M isn't fastest to $100M (vid: eq1bnI0JoXU — "8 entrepreneurs compete for $100K")

You can spike revenue with random discounts and still have the wrong business. That's why the show has an adherence round. Building it right matters as much as building it fast.

### Split the work, keep the architect (vid: uaLNfijnp-8 — "Scale or Fail: Hussle, dentistry")

Six visits per case; only three need the owner-doctor. Move the rest to associates and you double capacity from 25 to 50 a month without changing anything else. Position him as the architect and conductor, with a filmed handoff video. Identity shift: best dentist becomes best teacher of dentists.

### Nail it before you scale it (vid: uaLNfijnp-8 — "Scale or Fail: Hussle, dentistry")

Owners who aren't making enough assume they need more locations. Usually they need to make the current one better, until there's so much money they don't know where to put it. Then scale. Three sound locations might then be $45M, instead of three to reach $10M.

### Get the owner's time to zero before scaling, because 20% is not safe (vid: A8p2HCI_23E — "Answers Your Questions")

If a leader spends 20% of their time on a unit, a 5x puts them at 100% and everything caps. Hence the push to zero first; same trap for the one-location owner spending half their week inside the shop and wanting a second site.
For a young service business: list every daily action, bucket them into the workflows that create the customer outcome (there may be dozens), document, then automate one at a time. Don't add headcount for its own sake. A new founder's lightness is the advantage over a company carrying 200 people.

### Franchise or own: four-variable check (vid: BHMeYaHEMpc — "Grow ANY Local Business")

1. Return on capital: under about 100% a year per location usually favors franchising. 2. Who does the work: decentralized favors franchising, since you can open many more for the same effort. 3. Reverse the exit goal into locations at each model's multiple. 4. Founder type: promotional people enjoy selling franchises, product and ops people suit owned sites. If the math and personality clash, hire for the gap: a franchise-sales hunter for an ops founder, or a multi-unit operator for a promoter. Frame what you dislike as a fixable hole in the business.

### Where a service business reinvests without capex (vid: 8C_6qojTA78 — "Helping E-Commerce Owners Scale")

Brick and mortar opens a location, manufacturing buys a machine. Services have two sinks: talent and culture, and brand. Your price tells you how good you are. If you cannot charge more than rivals, you are not better yet. Brand money goes to big demonstrations outside normal business, such as Red Bull's jump from space, that keep people talking.

### Hand the ads off in four steps: founder, both, separate, successor (vid: HKbFUWJwEG0 — "Reverse Engineered the Perfect Business")

Brand is association, so transfer it. He filmed every session with his GM copying the same scripts. Run his ads, then the two together, then each alone, then only the GM. The GM's ads were worse at first and later outperformed Hormozi's. It took about 12 months. Keep any personal brand separate from the asset you may sell.

### Replace a founder with a Hydra of subject matter experts (vid: HKbFUWJwEG0 — "Reverse Engineered the Perfect Business")

No one person has lived your life, but several have each lived a slice. Brand one as the sales expert, one retention, one marketing, edify them to customers, step yourself down from you, to you plus them, to them. Those most edified can get small equity (0.1 to 0.5%, not 10%), which also makes non-competes hold. He ran a 90-minute Q&A daily for the first 400 days of Gym Launch and still got out.

### Unscalable is a skill gap: the 1:1 to 1:30 step-down (vid: H_ZLs1-jOKQ — "Stop Being Broke. Get Busy.")

Scale the Unscalable, with the steps. A woman at $30K a month on outbound wanted something more scalable; his answer was that unless physics forbids it, she just doesn't know how yet. Do 1:1 onboarding and note the six questions everyone asks, build a deck from them, try it 1:6 and check the output holds, then 1:30, then teach someone else the deck. Six 1:30 sessions is 180 people a day from one person. A portfolio company kept group onboarding past $100M a year. Andy Frisella's handwritten cards went from writing to signing to staff writing, and the gesture still held.

### Start at the hypothetical max, then find the 20% that does the work (vid: H_ZLs1-jOKQ — "Stop Being Broke. Get Busy.")

Gary Halbert's exercise as Hormozi tells it: imagine you could write only one letter in your life. Hand-address it, lick the stamp, handwrite the inside, drop in something lumpy. Do that for your first 100. The sales fund a system and the skill, then ask which elements drove the effect and keep them at 10x output for about 80% of the effect. Small companies should run the least scalable, most effective version: it is the one thing large rivals cannot copy.

### Pick a structure that improves with each customer (vid: Lc8DNduiwKA — "13 Ways to DESTROY Your Competition")

A one-on-one service gets worse with each added client, as most businesses do. A moat flips that: at a dentist buying group, each new member adds leverage over vendors. His own example is ALAN, where message volume cut the cost per message, so margins rise and a later entrant pays more. Ask whether customer number 1,000 helps or hurts customer number one.

### The vertical integration ladder (vid: Lc8DNduiwKA — "13 Ways to DESTROY Your Competition")

Walk your chain up one step at a time. For his books: self-publish, then own warehouses, presses, paper mills, forests. Each step eats a middleman's margin, raises quality control, and removes the risk of a vendor failing you. Private equity loves it because capital can buy it.

### Current revenue says little; the math says where you land (vid: FXzDLLdxsCk — "Revenue without more marketing")

Same inputs, 10 sales a month at $5K with 20% churn, cap at $250K. A business at $100K grows toward it; a business at $500K shrinks toward it. Same model, opposite stories. Work out which side you are on before reading growth as success. Figures are worked by him on screen, so check against the formula.

### Fix churn before you add sales (vid: FXzDLLdxsCk — "Revenue without more marketing")

To go from $250K to $1M: 4x sales, 4x price, or cut churn from 20% to 5%. Price is the least likely. If you scale sales first you build infrastructure too fast, margins compress, and churn stays. Fix the back end first, and then 4x sales takes you to $4M.

### Leverage you can pull without any AI (vid: WttZ6b-KUP4 — "Why AI won't make you rich")

One-on-one to one-to-ten is 10x with one decision. Scheduled to asynchronous client time. Educating prospects through the sale so two reps do what ten did. AI didn't cancel capital, media or teams, and they multiply each other.

### Study your cleanest launch and copy the checklist (vid: SasEJE4FI-I — "Your Failures Can Make You Rich")

Prestige Labs was his cleanest launch, $1.7M recurring in month one. What differed: operator on board, team trained and staffed, inventory ready, an alpha then a beta to work out kinks, and he let the team set the date. Leila's reminder: it took about a year of work and resources, so that is the price of the next one. Knowing how long one thing takes to do well tells you how few things you can do.

### When it's working, delay the next business and triple spend (vid: ULGT0Qpglek — "17 Life-Changing Conversations")

Doubling monthly at a private event of eight-figure owners, he planned a supplement company. A peer asked what would happen if he just spent three times more on marketing instead. He held the supplement business for 18 months and went from about $400K a month to $780K, to $1M, to $2M within about six months, by his telling. Another at that event: fat pitches come maybe two or three times in life, so when it gets easy is when to go hard. After product market fit, innovate on volume, not on a new win.

### The two-sided funnel: customers on one side, talent on the other (vid: aBPWCdaJJqA — "I Helped 6 Business Owners")

$1K to $1M is skills, $1M to $100M is character, and he guesses $100M to $1B is time. Speed of scaling tracks speed of recruiting; only knowing how to attract customers means things keep breaking and you can't climb out. Goldman and McKinsey pull top talent because the brand and training let people go anywhere after. Skill order for building yourself: something to sell, then promotion, then conversion, then leadership and operations.

### From business to conglomeration past $30M (vid: okA9Yt2KZuk — "How I Scaled My Sales Team")

Hormozi attributes it to Mark Ford's Ready, Fire, Aim: stuck around $30M means you are the only fuel, and by the fourth org layer staff don't know you or your vision. His fix: business units with entrepreneurial owners of their own P&Ls, paid on their line's growth ($300K-$500K a year if they crush it, or they'd start their own), around a shared hub of IT, HR, legal and finance. Hire people who have run it, not people you plan to train.
