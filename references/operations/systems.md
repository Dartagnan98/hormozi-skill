## Simple Operational Framework (Activities + Time + Inflow)

### Step 1: List desired outcomes
- Think of business as pipeline: click -> opt-in -> schedule -> show -> buy -> resell
- Identify all activities required at each stage

### Step 2: Calculate time per activity per customer
- Example: 2 minutes/client/day for coaching check-ins
- At 100 clients, that's 3 hours 20 minutes/day

### Step 3: Block time on calendars
- Take activities + allocated time and place on each role's calendar
- This IS operations: activities, units of time, expected inflow
- "That's honestly all there is to it"

### Step 4: Calculate breaking points
- Know at what inflow level each role becomes overwhelmed
- Know when you need to create a new role

### Step 5: Time study for baseline
- Excel sheet with 15-minute increments
- Employee writes what they did each 15 minutes
- "You will be more efficient than when you do a time study. I promise."
- Reveals inefficiencies, padded roles, unnecessary activities

### Key insight on finding inefficiency:
- Many employees spend time on activities that aren't valuable
- Not malicious -- they just don't understand the bigger picture
- "A lot of people just don't know what's valuable"

## CEO to Owner Transition Framework

### The Identity Shift
- CEO = full-time role running the company
- Owner = strategic adviser, quarterly meetings, "nose in, hands out"
- NIHO principle: "Nose in, hands out" -- attend meetings but don't walk away with to-dos

### Litmus Test
- 6-month test: Does the company grow without your involvement?
- Not just maintain -- GROW in both top line and profit
- "The company has to be able to grow and innovate without you"

### Communication Structure
- Remove yourself from company Slack/Asana
- Create separate communication center for executives only
- Portfolio companies: one Slack with all executives, teams access their executives

### The Two-Step Forward Method (Active to Passive)
- Instead of taking one step forward (delegate current work, do next thing)...
- Take TWO steps forward: delegate current work AND get someone to do the next thing
- You step back to owner role while two people do both levels of work
- Your income may decrease but enterprise value increases

### Key Beliefs
- "You get paid on what you do, you get returns on what you own"
- Business that required you at $17M profit: not sellable (personality brand)
- Same business without you at $8M profit: far more valuable as enterprise
- "Poor people do the wrong things, rich people do the right things, wealthy people own the right things"

## Business That Runs Without You (5-Step Framework)

### Step 1: Self-Inventory
- List everything you do (literally all of it)
- Run a time study: 15-minute intervals, write one word for what you did
- "It'll be the most productive week of your life"
- Can do this with key employees too (key-man risk reduction)

### Step 2: Build Processes
- For each item: assign Project (one-time), Process (ongoing), or Person
- Color code: Green (can give to someone now), Yellow (need to build process first), Red (need person you don't have)
- Solve green first, then yellow, then red
- Create if-then decision trees for common scenarios
- Set approval thresholds (e.g., under $X, they can act independently)

### Step 3: Scorecards + KPIs
- Ask every person: "How do you in your role make the company money?"
- Tie every function to the bottom line
- Even ancillary roles: camera person -> bad work wastes team time, wastes footage, wrong equipment
- Reverse potential mistakes into measurable KPIs

### Step 4: Test and Graduate
- Shadow-train: they watch you do it
- Supervise: they do it in front of you
- Support independence: available but not involved
- "I'm cool with 80%, sometimes 60%, as long as I have a clear path to them getting better"
- Measure employees on rate of improvement, not starting skill level

### Step 5: Remove Yourself from Marketing
- 7 ways to generate marketing without founder's face:
  1. Screenshot customer praise from communities weekly -> ads
  2. Incentivize testimonials (unlock higher service tiers as reward)
  3. Lifecycle ads: pull recordings from sales call -> onboarding -> delivery -> testimonial
  4. Document key client deliverable moments (reveals, results, celebrations)
  5. Pay customer support $5 per mini-testimonial captured
  6. Turn Google/Yelp reviews into marketing (including funny 1-stars)
  7. Build affiliate program / TikTok shop

### The Phone Test
- "Can you take 3 months off and have the business grow?"
- If your phone doesn't ring and business is bigger when you return: you have a real business
- Overexpansion is #1 reason people go out of business

### Key Beliefs to Adopt
- "If I can do it right with part of my time, someone else can do it better with all of their time"
- "You can outwork anyone in your business. You will not be able to outwork everyone."
- A players are worth 5x B players (Harvard study)

## The What-How-Who Framework (Scaling Yourself)
*From Andy Grove's "High Output Management"*

### Three Questions When Someone Isn't Performing
1. **Do they know WHAT I want them to do?** (Communication)
   - Rule: "If it's not written, it never happened"
   - Follow up verbal directives with Slack/email/Asana
2. **Do they know HOW to do it?** (Training)
   - Provide training, course, hop on a call, have another employee train them
3. **Are they MOTIVATED to do it?** (Motivation)
   - Sub-reasons for lack of motivation:
     - Don't understand WHY it's important in larger company context
     - Feel like their work doesn't matter (meaningless/busy work)
     - Not bought into company vision/mission
   - "When you scale, what you scale is culture and training"

### Diagnosing Missing Elements
- Has WHAT + motivation but not HOW: pretends to be busy (ashamed they don't know)
- Has HOW + motivation but not WHAT: executes on irrelevant things, not aligned with priorities
- Has WHAT + HOW but not motivation: checks out, avoids confrontation, probably job hunting

## The Talent Grid (What-How-Who Applied to Departments)

### Three Levels of Competence

| Level | Focus | Role | Activity |
|-------|-------|------|----------|
| 1 | WHAT to do | Individual Contributor | Doing |
| 2 | HOW to do it | Manager | Teaching |
| 3 | WHO can do it | Leader | Systematizing |

### Level 1: Individual Contributor (What)
- You have to get things done yourself
- All time spent doing
- Developing base skills, becoming proficient

### Level 2: Manager (How)
- Understand the sequence of how things work
- Can teach others to replicate the skill
- Hold them accountable to consistent execution
- Note: managers don't need to be better than individual contributors at the task

### Level 3: Leader (Who)
- "Building the machine that builds the machine"
- First you do it, then you teach it, then you build something that teaches it for you
- This is what cracks open $1M+/month level
- Systematizing, not just delegating

### Department Grading
- Apply the grid to each department (marketing, sales, product, shared services)
- Grade yourself: where am I involved?
- Goal: move above all departments as CEO with leaders who build machines
- "If I'm not on the Slack channel... that is what I believe is a truly delegated business"
- Your strengths often become the bottleneck (you're comfortable delegating what you don't know)

### Key Insight from Reid Hoffman
- Most employees struggle to jump between levels
- Rapid-growth companies often change out leadership
- Good individual contributors are often terrible leaders (and vice versa)
- Takes a unique person to master all levels (why being an operator is so hard)

## Maker vs Manager Time

### Manager Schedule
- Divide time into smallest chunks possible (15 min to 90 min)
- Empty time slot = lost opportunity
- Fully booked day = maximally productive
- Day duration = first meeting prep to last meeting end

### Maker Schedule
- Only large chunks (4-6 hours)
- Only have ~2-3 blocks per day, ~10 blocks per week
- Empty calendar IS the opportunity
- One meeting in the morning kills 10% of weekly output
- Set start time but variable end time (work open to goal)
- A maker's "no" to a meeting is them keeping their commitment to meaningful work

### The Conflict
- Manager putting one meeting in = one of their 20+ daily slots
- Same meeting for maker = one of their 10 weekly blocks (10% of total output)
- Managers prevent the work they check in on
- When maker falls behind, manager's solution is to interrupt more (vicious cycle)

### Three-Pronged Solution

**For Managers:**
1. Understand the cost: you disrupt them TWICE (coordinating + the meeting itself)
2. Understand the maker's "no" -- it's not personal, it's productivity
3. Ask teams what ideal day looks like and follow it

**For Makers:**
1. Tell managers how you work (share this framework)
2. Have standard meeting times (e.g., Monday and Thursday afternoons)
3. Communicate when you'll be slow to respond
4. Actually work during maker time (don't prove suspicions right)

**For Organizations:**
1. Mandate quiet time (specific days or time blocks)
2. Wednesday quiet day at Acquisition.com (organization-wide)
3. Separate people into makers vs managers
4. Audit meetings: "Do I need to do this again? Is there a process/person instead?"

### Hormozi's Personal Calendar
- 4 maker days per week, 1 manager day (Mondays/Tuesdays)
- Manager day: "Just destroy me. Put everything in there."
- Maker days: completely empty except gym
- Leila: inverted (4 manager days, 1 maker day + weekends for maker work)

## System for Outworking Everyone

### Redefining Work
- Work = Outputs = Volume x Leverage
- Not force x distance, not time on clock, not effort expended
- "How much money you get out for the time you put in"

### The 4 Growth Levers
1. Get more customers (more traffic OR higher conversion)
2. Make customers worth more (raise prices OR decrease COGS OR get them to buy more times)

### Activities That Map to Levers
- More traffic: run ads, do outreach, make content, use affiliates (4 hours/day)
- Higher conversion: track close rate, watch game tape, practice scripts daily, pre-sale questionnaires
- "If you look at your schedule and you're not laddering up to these four things, you're not doing the stuff that makes money"

### Finding Time: Buying Back 96 Hours/Month
- Food/groceries/prep/cleaning: 50 hours/month -> $800 to replace
- House cleaning: 25 hours/month -> $500 to replace
- Laundry: 16 hours/month -> $200 to replace
- Total: 96 hours/month for $1,500/month
- "That's two full work weeks every single month"
- If you make more than $15/hour, this trade is obvious
- One-time investment -- can only pull this trigger once

### Free Time to Reclaim
- Following sports: 4 hours/week (cost to eliminate: $0)
- Watching TV: 21 hours/week ($0)
- Scrolling social media: 17 hours/week ($0)

### The 30x Multiplier
- Someone doing 96 productive hours/week vs someone doing 3 productive hours/week
- "That's how someone can move 30 times faster than you"
- Both people "work all day" -- the difference is what counts as real work

### Habits That Served Hormozi
- Wake up 4am, work until 9am (first 8 years of career)
- Decide the one project night before (never more than 3 things)
- Dark room, no windows, earplugs, no notifications
- "There are no emergencies. There's 911 and there's your business not growing for the third year."
- Same bedtime every night including weekends
- Set alarm for bedtime, not wake-up time
- Weekends = 104 days/year of uninterrupted time

## MAKER-MANAGER CALENDAR SYSTEM (Deep Pass Batch 2 -- Expanded)

### V1 (Starting Out): Maker Time in Off-Hours
- 5-9am before work, both weekend days
- = 9 maker blocks/week
- Best for solopreneurs still in a day job or running operations

### V2 (Intermediate): 50/50 Split
- First half of day = maker, second half = manager
- Schedule meetings back-to-front (avoids dead time between meetings)
- Ensures makers get uninterrupted morning blocks

### V3 (Advanced): Dedicated Days
- 4 maker days, 1 manager day (Alex's schedule)
- Or inverted: 4 manager days, 1 maker day (Leila's schedule)
- Organization-wide quiet day (Wednesday at acquisition.com)
- Manager day: "Just destroy me. Put everything in there."
- Maker days: completely empty except gym

### Pro Tips
- At end of every meeting, ask "Do I need to do this again?" and "Is there a person or system that can replace this?"
- Zeigarnik effect: open loops (pending meetings) eat brain power even before they happen
- A single meeting in a maker's morning destroys 10% of their weekly output (one of ~10 blocks)
- For a manager, same meeting is 1 of 100+ slots -- costs maker 10x
- When makers fall behind, manager's instinct is to check in MORE -> creates vicious cycle

### The Algorithm, in order (vid: ZTSI3DDP_4A — "Social strategy 2027, The Algorithm")

From Tesla's playbook: question requirements, delete (so much you must add some back), simplify (most for least), accelerate, then automate. Only elimination beats automation. Anyone can add back a step, so deletion is rarely a one-way door. He made this required reading for his direct reports.

### Time on task exposes waste (vid: ZTSI3DDP_4A — "Social strategy 2027, The Algorithm")

If the work takes 10 hours and delivery takes a week, everything outside the 10 hours is uncaptured value. Ask: how much actual time does this take? Then: why must these steps happen in this order and take this long?

### AI drafts, humans approve (vid: 8VqSFBMMS4M — "Scale or Fail: Tina, hate selling")

Have an agent search the whole community history for similar questions and propose answers in a dashboard your team approves or edits. Result as reported: from about 30 hours a week of handholding to 3 to 5.

### Put a cone in the road (vid: TLgSkSQUql4 — "Answering your sales questions live")

Asking low-skill staff to follow a step gets poor adherence unless there is a big carrot or stick. Better: make the step unavoidable. The onboarding or discharge flow on screen cannot be completed until the ask has been made.
Case: adolescent behavioral health provider, about $12M a year, flat 8 months, roughly 3 months of runway, 60-70% census needing to be near 80%, and only a handful of reviews across 6 locations. Staff refused to ask, citing ethics. He did not buy it and reframed: more families finding help, less shame. The script is only two sentences.

### The six-column goal sheet Acquisition.com runs in every company (vid: 9OsUfwTTslc — "Goal setting 1/0 live Q&A")

Hormozi's quarterly and annual planning format. Column 1: which problem does this solve, and it must chunk up to more customers, more money per customer, or lower risk of losing either. Column 2: the hypothesis. Column 3: X, the input you will do. Column 4: Y, the output you expect.
At period end add two more: did we actually do X, and did Y show up? Did X but no Y means the hypothesis was wrong, so amend it (doubling videos and losing quality means the new input is doubling videos while keeping scripts as good).
Bonus use: when an employee pitches a goal, ask which of the three buckets it serves. If they can't say, drop it.

### Airline delay method for long lead times (vid: ehmBlLNGgsA — "Focus is subtraction live Q&A")

Custom car parts maker, three weeks or more, customers get antsy even with lead times posted. Copy airlines: more communication cycles, stated facts, and an update even when the update is that nothing changed. It shows you watch their order more closely than they do. It can be automated, so it is one-time work at no real cost.

### Measurement as intervention (vid: 4GQLJjH9-oA — "This Business Was Stuck")

Just tracking changes behavior: have someone weigh themselves and they lose weight; have them report profit and it goes up. Report show, schedule and close rates on a cadence short enough to act on. The stat you hate looking at, look at twice.

### If stats fall on handoff, you abdicated (vid: 4GQLJjH9-oA — "This Business Was Stuck")

Delegation should hold or lift stats, since someone equally skilled now has more time. A drop means you handed off without preparation or checklist, so stay beside them until it holds. Founders who drift too far from revenue stop knowing where the bodies are buried and can't add value. His founder hadn't role-played discovery with the team at all.

### Workflow thinking replaces role thinking (vid: 9q5ojtkqsBs — "How to Win With AI")

Before any hire, list the 4 to 10 things that person physically does, then ask if each could be a workflow instead of headcount. Old: I need an editor. New: which five steps make a video, and each one is a workflow. Org charts exist to manage humans; lay the tasks under the boxes in one line, inputs to output, like a factory, and automate one task at a time.

### Train agents the way you should train people (vid: 9q5ojtkqsBs — "How to Win With AI")

People judge an agent on one bad output, which they would never do to a new hire. Strip feeling words like charisma or "make it lighter" and define what good looks like in observable terms. His copy example: a fixed set of rules (he says 12) plus samples of your writing (he says 16) beats a bare prompt by a wide margin. The feedback loop that takes a person a year and a half can take minutes with AI, about 100 cycles either way.

### First-step AI audit (vid: 9q5ojtkqsBs — "How to Win With AI")

Write your day at task level, not "I run ads" but budgets, copy, creative, headline tests. Put the first task into AI, ask how to automate it, do step one of the answer. Stuck? Screenshot the screen and ask what to do next. He also recommends one weekend building a single agent: hands-on beats any amount of reading about it. Inside a company, spend about 20% of your time automating your own job.

### Core vs ancillary functions (vid: BYpTRiRqS1Y — "4 Educational Business Owners")

Only three functions create value in any business: attraction, conversion, delivery. IT, recruiting and finance must happen but aren't core, so those are what you can outsource. A personal brand sits in attraction and is the main asset: hire the brand manager in-house. To find one, look up who runs media behind brands you admire and offer them more.

### Finance in three levels, and the feeling that means it is missing (vid: HKbFUWJwEG0 — "Reverse Engineered the Perfect Business")

Level 1: any books, outsourced bookkeeper. Level 2: accountant, moving from cash to GAAP (a $30K three-month contract is $10K a month, not $30K today). Level 3: audit-ready, so a third party's quality of earnings matches your profit; he calls it a blue check, and says your own accountant may flatter the number. Manufacturing needs a stronger finance arm than light services. A CEO who went from a couple of million a year to a month asked where the profit was going and how many locations he could open. That uncertainty is the pattern for no finance function, same as not knowing where the next customer comes from means no marketing function.

### Plateaus are basics that stopped at scale (vid: IMowPVgcWbA — "SIMPLE Managerial Framework")

Small businesses grow on personal attention and service with a smile. They hire, smiles and skill drop, and growth stops. Ask what made you win early and how to duplicate exactly that at volume, then inspect it as a report.

### Cloud to dirt: why the owner has to learn the AI tools (vid: fr78adfAnuA — "How to Use AI in Your Business in 2026")

You don't become an AI business, just as you didn't become an internet one, and you don't advertise it: customers care about faster, cheaper, better, less risk. But a hired tech person only knows what they've seen and defaults to commodity automations. Big gains come from overlaying business knowledge on technical knowledge, so the owner has to know top to bottom what's possible.

### How a non-technical owner learns: automate one workflow end to end (vid: fr78adfAnuA — "How to Use AI in Your Business in 2026")

Find a YouTube video on automating the task, give its transcript to any AI, say you want help building it, and follow the steps. Stuck? Screenshot the page and ask what to do now. Automate your own workflow yourself to free time for higher value work. Where you'd say gut or instinct, read it as pattern recognition and write out the observable steps.

### AI support, examples he cites (vid: fr78adfAnuA — "How to Use AI in Your Business in 2026")

His book launch: five agents resolved about 90% of tickets without a human, mostly "where's my free stuff". He also cites Klarna replacing 700 agents and saving $40M in a year, and JPMorgan's contract tool saving 350,000 lawyer hours (from memory, not verified). Legal idea: one general counsel runs agents for first responses and cease and desists rather than a paralegal bench.

### AI-first means data-first (vid: jqo0lVveh98 — "If You Have a Service Business")

Using a chatbot for emails doesn't make you AI first. AI works on data, so first capture every part of the business in one architecture, then layer AI and train it by reinforcement. His comparison for the moment: the shift from software on CDs to cloud. The pieces not yet fitting together is where the money gets made.

### Local cost versus global benefit: the $70,000 automation (vid: oK2_u-uS_Bo — "Money Lessons I Learned In Marriage")

A process costs someone locally, so it must pay globally. CRM notes pass if support, onboarding and product use them; if nobody uses them, stop asking. His case: accounting wanted a tool to save 4 hours a week. Fourteen dev days at $5,000 a day is $70,000; about 200 hours a year is 10% of a $70,000 salary, or $7,000. Ten years to pay back, assuming nothing changes. The best operators are delegation and accountability machines, not "systems people": under $10M, hire people you can trust with shared values, because strict rules are what you need for weak people.

### Beta process: product innovation without the guru (vid: okA9Yt2KZuk — "How I Scaled My Sales Team")

Taken from management consulting; he says every PE firm they talked to loved it. Clients upvote their worst problems. Pick the top performers on the top problem (10-20 people; top 1% of his 1,000 locations) and run a free round table in exchange for their secrets. Write down everything, then keep only what all of them do, not what any of them does: a churn list of about 180 items came down to five. Productize it, give it to bottom 10% and middle clients through the normal portal with no extra help, and measure at 30 days. Works: ship it. Fails: publish the failed test, still valuable. Either way a process, not the founder, drives innovation, which makes the business sellable.

### Replace-yourself-with-AI challenge (vid: z7X95bn2T6A — "Team Replace Themselves with AI")

He gave every department a week. Scoring: two hours a day saved on a 40 hour week is 25% more output at the same cost. Uses, more durable than the tools named: one recorded video with AI swapping in each prospect's name to lift show rates; email follow-ups drafted right after calls; a voice clone trained on about 10 minutes for audio versions and voice memos; editing video by its transcript; avatar onboarding videos where you edit text instead of reshooting, so the training stays current; note bots on interviews (10-15 minutes saved per call) and internal meetings (notes read in about a tenth of the time). His case for protecting founder time: a 30 second recording ask can cost him 30 minutes to get back on track.

### Goal sheet: worked example and pushing it down the org (vid: zBZHWrvjD8Y — "Set Goals That Make Money")

Extends the quarterly planning format. Illustrative: lead response 37 minutes to 5, expecting schedule rate 50% to 70%. They reached 7 minutes with no lift, so no reason to chase 5: no directional gain means change the hypothesis. A videographer does it too: double shorts for more site clicks; shorts doubled, clicks didn't; next hypothesis, more story-based content. Taking a day a quarter is the point: if goals come fast, you haven't thought. Staff who can't tie their work to the company's goals lose motivation.
