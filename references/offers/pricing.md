## The Virtuous Cycle of Premium Pricing

### Why You Should Charge More, Not Less

Hormozi's argument against competing on price:

"There is a very large, deep, dark, dangerous chasm between 'Cheap' and 'Premium' pricing. One in which many businesses die."

The commoditization trap:
- You lower price to compete
- Lower price = lower margins = less money for fulfillment
- Worse fulfillment = worse results = worse reputation
- Worse reputation = fewer customers = need to lower price more
- Death spiral

The premium pricing virtuous cycle:
- You charge premium prices
- Higher margins = more money for fulfillment
- Better fulfillment = better results = better reputation
- Better reputation = more referrals = can charge even more
- Growth spiral

## Price-to-Value Discrepancy

"The goal of a Grand Slam Offer is to create such a discrepancy between price and value that the prospect would be a fool to say no."

If your offer stacks up to $10,000 in perceived value and you charge $2,000, that's a 5:1 value-to-price ratio. The prospect thinks: "I'm getting $10K of stuff for $2K."

Target: 10:1 value-to-price ratio minimum.

"Price is what you pay. Value is what you get. Our job is to make value dwarf price."

## Low-Price to High-Price Migration

### Starting Free or Cheap to Build Flow

"Start with low prices or even free. The reason is that if you start there, you can go up. It's very difficult to come down."

Hormozi's personal example:
- First business: Online personal training
- Charged $0 -- asked clients to donate to charity of their choice ($500-$1,000)
- Got first 13 customers this way
- After 12 weeks of great results, asked: "Would you be willing to pay me now?"
- "Yeah, I'm the charity now"
- Used results and referrals to raise prices from there

### The Three-Phase Migration

1. **Phase 1: Free/charity/low-cost** -- Get testimonials, case studies, proof. Learn fulfillment.
2. **Phase 2: Market-rate pricing** -- Now you have proof. Charge what competitors charge.
3. **Phase 3: Premium pricing** -- Now you have superior proof. Charge 5-10x what competitors charge.

"Each phase funds the credibility needed for the next phase."

## Pricing Decision Framework

- **Cost-plus pricing** (BAD): "It costs me $X to deliver, so I'll charge $X + margin"
- **Market-based pricing** (MEDIOCRE): "Competitors charge $Y, so I'll charge slightly less/more"
- **Value-based pricing** (GOOD): "This delivers $Z in value to the customer, so I'll charge a fraction of $Z"

"Never compete on price. If the only difference between you and a competitor is price, you've already lost."

"Charge as much money for your products or services as humanly possible."

"If you are not embarrassed by your price, you're not charging enough."

"The commoditized business owner who tries to get more customers is doing so at the mercy of larger advertising budgets."

## Money Models: Maximizing Cash Flow from Pricing

### Definition

A money model is a deliberate sequence of offers structured to:
1. Generate the most upfront cash
2. Get the most leads to convert
3. Maximize lifetime value
4. Accelerate cash flow timing

"Most businesses spend money to get customers and then HOPE to make it back over time. That's playing on hard mode. Money Models flips that -- you make more money getting a customer than it costs to get them within the first 30 days."

### The Four Offer Types in a Money Model

**1. Attraction Offers** -- Maximize likelihood of conversion (getting leads to buy)
- Tend to have upfront cash components that pull cash flow forward
- Goal: get the most people through the door

**2. Upsells** -- Maximize gross profit per customer
- Once someone buys, what else can they buy?
- "People have different wallets" -- gym membership wallet, supplement wallet, apparel wallet, food wallet
- Each is a separate buying decision with separate budget

**3. Downsells** -- Turn nos into yeses
- Capture customers who won't pay for the premium but will pay for something
- CRITICAL: Must be presented so that people who would have bought the premium still do
- Bad downsell: cannibalizes your premium offer
- Good downsell: only captures incremental sales from people who were leaving

**4. Continuity** -- Get customers to buy again and again
- Transforms one-time transactions into ongoing relationships
- "We don't get customers to make sales. We make sales to get customers."

### Money Model Case Study: Gym Industry Disruption

**Competitor's model:**
- $21 for 21-day trial (attraction offer)
- 25% conversion of 100 leads = 25 customers x $21 = $525 upfront
- 35% convert to $99/month membership (continuity) = 8 customers
- 8 customers x 5-month average retention x $99 = $3,960
- Total from 100 leads: $525 + $3,960 = ~$5,325
- Max spend per lead: $53
- Problem: Takes 5+ months to recoup ad spend

**Hormozi's model:**
- Free 6-week challenge (attraction offer -- gets more leads at lower cost)
- 15% conversion at $500 upfront = 15 customers x $500 = $7,500 (day 1)
- Supplement upsell: 12 of 15 buy $200 avg = $2,400 (day 1-2)
- Rollover upsell: Credit $500 toward annual membership at $200/month = 70% take rate = 10 customers
- 10 customers x 5 months x $200 = $10,000 (over 5 months)
- Prepay discount at week 6: 20% of remaining buy annual = ~$5,000 (week 6)
- Total from 100 leads: $7,500 + $2,400 + $10,000 + $5,000 = $24,900
- Max spend per lead: $249
- Result: **4.7x more revenue from the same 100 leads**
- Almost all cash collected in first 30 days

"The guys I was competing against sold the same product as me. Maybe my trainers were better, maybe my location was a little better. But fundamentally, I won because I had a better money model."

### Money Model Mechanics (Specific Offer Types Used)

From the gym case study, four named mechanics:
1. **Win-your-money-back offer** (attraction) -- Free/low-cost entry that front-loads a high-ticket sale at the point of maximum motivation
2. **You-can't-buy-X-without-Y upsell** (classic upsell) -- Supplements immediately after gym purchase, hitting a different wallet
3. **Rollover upsell** (upsell + continuity) -- Crediting money already paid toward a longer commitment ("Let me put your $500 toward the annual")
4. **Buy-X-get-Y prepay** (continuity acceleration) -- "Buy 10 months, get 2 free" to lock in cash and extend retention

### Key Money Model Principles

1. **Front-load cash collection** -- Get as much money as possible in the first 48 hours
2. **Different wallets** -- People have separate mental budgets for different categories. Sell into each wallet.
3. **Credit rollover** -- Use money already paid as leverage for the next offer ("Let me credit your $500 toward the annual")
4. **The business that can spend the most to acquire a customer wins** -- Money models are about maximizing this number
5. **Stack offer types** -- Use multiple attraction, upsell, downsell, and continuity offers in sequence
6. **Outspend competitors into oblivion** -- "In an auction of attention, the person who can outspend has an ethical and legal monopoly of attention"
7. **Point of greatest pain = point of greatest sale** -- "When someone signs up, their highest point of motivation is the moment they walk in the door. That is when they're the most deprived of the outcome. That is the point I'm not trying to sell someone something for $20. I'm trying to sell them something for $500."

## PRICING PLAYBOOKS (Deep Pass)

### Anchor Upsell Playbook
- Learned from suit shop: budgeted $500, saw $16,000 Armani suit first, ended up spending $2,500-3,000
- Rolls-Royce stopped showing at car shows, started showing at plane/yacht shows
- Anchor must be 5-10x the price of main offer (not 20% more)
- Steps: Present anchor -> Get the gasp -> Come to rescue -> Present main offer -> Ask for payment
- If they DON'T gasp: close the whale
- One out of 10 buying 10x product = doubles your business
- Change only secondary features between anchor and main offer (keep primary features the same)

### The Gasp Test
- "If you didn't get a gasp from the price, you didn't go high enough"
- A certain percentage of population ALWAYS buys the most expensive thing
- "Put it there with the expectation you're never going to sell and be surprised when you do"

### Skip-and-Anchor Method for Raising Prices
1. Want to go from $2K to $3K? Anchor at $4K (skip target price)
2. Sales team presents $4K as new price
3. "But you can prepay today for $3K" (the actual target)
4. Sales team still has $2K as emotional home base
5. Always clear the pipeline: announce price increase to create urgency before the change

### Price Increase Letter Format (Existing Customers)
1. Set the stage: "You've noticed we've been growing"
2. What we're currently doing (re-sell them on forgotten value)
3. What we want to do in the future (ambitious improvements)
4. Tie to values: "With integrity, I cannot maintain my promise unless we do this"
5. Communicate price in smallest unit (daily or weekly, not annual)
6. PS: "If this will materially affect your life, reach out"
- Execution: video AND direct mail, turn comments off

### Supply Control Pricing
- Chanel model: 1-2 bags per store, every bag is limited edition
- Create waiting list even if you're not at capacity
- Periodically "let off the steam" to capture demand at peak pricing
- "Being at full capacity is completely up to you -- you can say today that you are full"
- Marketing shifts the demand curve: more people know about you = higher prices

### Close Rate Diagnostics
- 60-80% = 3-5x price increase sitting there
- 50-60% = 2-3x available
- 30-50% = appropriately priced
- Below 30% = selling skill issue

### Pricing Architecture
1. 10x/50x/100x rule: create something at 10x, 50x, or 100x of base price
2. Price anchor high: show most expensive first
3. Medium price placement: $5/$9/$10 structure drives people to premium (not $5/$6/$10)
4. Match buying personas: $100/$200/$1000 (different people), not $100/$110/$120 (same person)
5. Value-based pricing: same service, different price based on customer revenue
6. Expect 1 in 20 to buy top tier

### Pricing Delivery Hack
- Before stating price: "Before I tell you the price, it's super expensive"
- Rich: think their version of expensive, your number seems fine
- Poor: brace themselves, less sticker shock
- Other: write price on paper, slide it. Or use calculator, turn screen to them

### Price Increase Raises Close Rates
- 50% price increase on health company -> close rates went UP
- "They were so cheap compared to the promise that people didn't even believe it worked"
- Wine experiment: same wine rated differently based on price label

## PRICING INSIGHTS (Batch 7)

### "They Can't Afford It" Is Always Wrong
- Ferrari test: If selling a brand new Ferrari for $10K, people would call friends, family, max credit cards, take loans
- "The reason someone is not buying is simply because they do not understand the value"
- "Everyone can afford it if they believe the value is there"

### Price Anchoring: The Gasp Test (Expanded)
- "If you didn't get a gasp from the price, you didn't go high enough"
- The gasp shows you've broken the "rubber band of pricing" they associated with your product
- A certain percentage of population ALWAYS buys the most expensive thing
- "Put it there with the expectation you're never going to sell and be surprised when you do"

### Frozen Yogurt Psychology Playbook (Applicable to Any Business)
1. **Self-serve pricing by usage:** Customers blame themselves, not you
2. **Remove small option:** Only offer medium and large (power of default) -- increased sales 20-30%
3. **Present highest-margin items first:** Yogurt (highest margin) -> dry toppings -> fresh fruit (lowest margin)
4. **Buy foreclosed equipment:** rasmus.com, 5-10 cents on the dollar
5. **Competitions for customer acquisition:** Fraternities, companies, affiliations competing against each other
6. **Text list capture:** "Save 50% today by joining" = $2 lead acquisition

### Career Year Pricing (Hotline Example)
- $30K ask, downsell to $15K down + $15K when kid gets job
- Credit the $4K workshop toward the $30K
- Pitch parents at "halfway call" (week 2-3): make it feel like phase 2 of existing process, not a new sale
- "You're going to split it with your kid -- they're becoming an adult now"
- Get third-party financing or do in-house payment plans

## PRICING FROM TOP DOWN (Deep Pass -- Batch 4)

### Tesla Roadster Approach
1. Start highest price first
2. Brand narrative works top-down: "Many couldn't afford this, so I made something more affordable"
3. Reverse doesn't work (budget brand going premium)
4. Fewer customers at high price = manageable operations
5. Add tiers one at a time, 5-10x each
6. Alex's tiers: $99/mo (School) -> $5K (L1) -> ~$35K (L2) -> $135K (L3) -> Portfolio (equity)

### Pareto on Steroids -- Upsell Math
- 20% of customers = 80% of profits
- 4% of customers = 64% of profits
- 1% of customers = 51% of profits
- Upsell rule: **5-10x price** per tier, expect **20% take rate**
- Example: 1,000 at $10/mo ($10K) + 200 at $100/mo ($20K) + 40 at $1,000/mo ($40K) + 8 at $10,000/mo ($80K) = $150K total from $10K base
- 15x revenue from adding 3 tiers to a base product

### Suffering Is a Fixed Cost
- Poverty is tough. Growth is tough. Being an employee is tough. Being rich is tough
- "What if all three paths have the same amount of suffering? Pick the biggest reward"
- "The goal is to reframe reality so that bad things are good, not to only experience good things"

### Passion = Suffering, Etymology
- Latin "passio" = suffering. First usage: Passion of Christ (crucifixion)
- 95% of daily work won't be "your passion" even in a passion-based business
- "You're using the excuse of a lack of passion to disguise your inability to handle difficulty"

## FROM $100M LOST CHAPTERS

### Pick Your Price Model (from Book)

**Two versions:**

**Version 1 (Value Ladder Pick Your Price):**
- Present multiple tiers with increasing value at increasing prices
- Each tier includes a guarantee
- Let the customer self-select based on perceived value and budget
- Example tiers: $0 (basic access), $50 (standard + guarantee), $200 (premium + VIP + guarantee)

**Version 2 (Supporter Labels):**
- Same product at every level, different identity labels
- Example: $0 (freeloader), $10 (supporter), $50 (champion), $100 (hero)
- Appeals to identity, status, and social belonging
- "Nobody wants to be labeled a freeloader"

**COVID Car Wash Story:**
- During COVID, car wash implemented pick-your-price
- Average ticket: **$99** (normal price was significantly lower)
- Massive goodwill, customer loyalty through the roof
- "When you trust people to pay what it's worth, most people pay MORE"

**Key insight:** Pick Your Price works best when there's already established goodwill or when the product has clear, demonstrable value. It fails when used as a desperation move or when value isn't obvious.

### Charge more for one-on-one to protect the funnel (vid: 8VqSFBMMS4M — "Scale or Fail: Tina, hate selling")

Checkout $2,500, or $3,000 if they need to talk to the owner, simply because it takes her time. Include a bonus only for buying on the call. Fewer DMs and more people buy immediately. If everyone pays the extra $500, raise prices. A $150 one-on-one add-on should be several times that.

### Cost basis, then 5x to 10x (vid: mRlSb0O5QNU — "Scale or Fail: Joey, event planning")

Stop inventing line items. Build a sheet: fixed costs, costs scaling with headcount, staff fractions. The number at the bottom stays hidden, then the price sits at 5 to 10x it. Where you land depends on the buyer's money. Same lawn costs more in a top zip code. AI can build it in hours and enables live quotes.

### Priority fee: same work, more margin (vid: o64cI6tebnU — "Scale or Fail: Caleb, roofing")

Supply constrained? Let people pay to go first. A customer paid an extra $300 of pure margin to move up a job, then bought the next one too. Example structure: a 20% priority fee, done by end of week or free. Anyone within an acceptable wait just moves back.

### 90% close means raise prices (vid: uaLNfijnp-8 — "Scale or Fail: Hussle, dentistry")

At 85 to 90% close, price is too low. Raised 20%, which he calls right-sizing since the money supply rose more. Close rates went up. Price signals risk and quality in high-stakes purchases (Veblen effect): nobody picks the $50 tattoo. Keep raising until the math stops working, even if closes dip.

### The pricing virtuous cycle (vid: uaLNfijnp-8 — "Scale or Fail: Hussle, dentistry")

Charge more, attract better customers, over-deliver, earn more profit, hire better talent, over-deliver more. Going up market compounds; competing down starves you of margin to deliver.

### Selling to the poor still needs a lot of money (vid: Uzr0gv4N4-s — "Change My Mind, Win $10,000")

Be premium or be the low-cost leader; the latter wins on volume and front-loaded inventory, like Walmart. Regulated or subsidized markets are an exception since the money is still coming from elsewhere. Most owners he meets are underpriced and selling to everyone.

### Price at the point of marginal cheapness when the goal isn't revenue (vid: A8p2HCI_23E — "Answers Your Questions")

Alex asked members of his community for $1M+ owners how cheap it could go before nobody believed it was legit. The answer was $1,000 a month, so that's the price, though he says revenue would have peaked at $5,000.
Two distinct prices worth finding: the revenue-maximizing one and the lowest credible one. If you want the room full, ask your buyers where cheap stops being believable.

### Can't afford talent? Same pitch, bigger number (vid: I0nCuqu7Cac — "Hotline: mental toughness and offers")

Accounting firm: pays accountants about $80K, bills about $200K each, closes four of five calls and can't find mid-level CPAs. Hormozi reads that as one problem: underpriced, so it can't pay above market.
Prescription: double or triple the price and offer those hires $150K. The test costs nothing because a supply-constrained firm can afford lost deals. Change nothing except the number at the end of the call. Worst case they say no, and you can always drop it back.
When the owner defended the value, Hormozi cut him off: don't sell me, just say the new number on calls.

### Stuck with capped prices: sell the plus, make margin from the floor (vid: DfXy_PzlC0o — "Get rich young, plus live Q&A")

Government-funded disability provider in Australia, 30% gross margin, price capped. Two moves, usable together.
1) Sell insurance plus: ask a sorting question up front, whether they want the best solution or only the free one. Even 10% paying for extras can double profit.
2) Create margin from the floor: zero cost budgeting, imagine no expenses and rebuild only what is required. Template the service around the problem customers share.
His goal was a $500K sale. Small businesses trade on SDE at about one to three times, usually structured: example $200K up front and $300K over time if results hold. Getting customers to stay two or three years grows it regardless, and may make it something you would rather keep.

### Add a 10x tier: tiny take rates still dominate profit (vid: 7TmfdhOwIzU — "Brutally Honest Advice")

Worked example: 100 buyers, $100 core offer at 40% margin, $1,000 premium. If 90 take the core and 10 take the premium, revenue about doubles, and he says roughly 3/4 of the profit comes from those 10.
The anchor only works if you say the number out loud and go quiet so they weigh it fully. Mumbling past it kills it. If they balk, pull out a few components and offer the version with 90% of the elements.
Even if nobody buys, the top price lifts conversion and perceived value of everything beneath it.

### Price falls across a customer's life: the ski lodge beer math (vid: 32fVmwvLmTM — "Reddit AMA")

The first beer after skiing is worth more than the third. If people will buy three at $1, that's $3. Two at $3 is $6. One at $7 is $7. So value is not flat within one buyer, which is the case against linear recurring pricing.
Charge onboarding or upfront fees while deprivation is highest; it also offsets CAC. Hormozi's gym flip: stop handing the most motivated person of the year a free trial, charge big on day one, then a much cheaper ongoing rate.
People may buy for weight loss and stay for friends, and they pay more for the first. Price each phase by the value it holds at that moment.

### Intent traffic price shops, interruption traffic doesn't (vid: _ooj0V4hpgw — "Hormozi Hotline caller breakdowns")

Same IV business. Search leads compare six shops, so the price stays pinned, and they are in survive mode. Meta buyers didn't wake up shopping, so sell them a thrive message and an offer with one added component at about a 30% premium; the added item is what makes two prices ethical. He breaks his one-channel rule here because the search channel takes little work once running.
Until the model works, treat ad spend as reps on the offer and sales motion. Break-even is fine at that stage, since the business is still an experiment.

### Price raise break-even: how far close rate can fall (extends high close means underpriced) (vid: OQf2Ba-Lp_4 — "$2.5M Business for a Stranger")

Corey's HVAC cleaning business at about $1.2M, 38% margin and 82% close. Hormozi's math: a 10% raise adds about 25% to net profit at the same volume, and he still makes more money so long as close stays above 65%. Run this before asking if a raise is scary. An earlier 23% raise had already lifted Corey's close rate: for a normal service, a higher price raised belief he could deliver, not status as with a luxury good. Biggest lever for most owners under about $3M to $5M.

### You are the worst judge of your price (vid: -Pz0NpcILOY — "Inverse thinking on pay")

What is easy for you is hard for others. Hormozi does not care if an oil change is $100 or $300; a fellow mechanic would call $200 crazy for two seconds of work. Your long practice distorts what it seems worth, so let the market set the number by testing higher asks.

### Pricing is a math problem: raise until conversion x price falls (vid: 41EvCgwPrDc — "Make More Profit than 99% of People")

Don't ask an advisor which price; compare conversion and LTV at each. Keep raising until revenue per sales conversation drops (ignoring hard costs; maximize total gross profit), then step back. Start low, because going up is easy to explain: more demand, better product. Going down hurts: if you overshoot, refund the difference or add a bonus for those who paid more. He ranks price as the biggest profit lever, though his multiples varied in this video (6x, then 3x vs more customers), so don't quote them.

### Three lines: cost, price, value (vid: 41EvCgwPrDc — "Make More Profit than 99% of People")

Below price sits profit (price minus cost); above it sits customer surplus (value minus price). People leave when value sinks under price. A course's value falls to zero once learned, so a flat monthly charge crosses that line by design. That's the reason to price one-time value upfront and only the consumable part monthly, even if it means $5,000 down and $200 to $300 a month.

### Price expectations lag cost collapse (vid: 9q5ojtkqsBs — "How to Win With AI")

Buyers adapt slowly, including on price. A $2,000 a month service has typical labor cost baked into what people expect to pay. Keep that price while AI strips the labor out and margin jumps. The bigger win is operating leverage: fewer people per added revenue dollar, since human coordination is one of the largest costs of scaling.

### Agency clients going in-house: price the switch to consulting (vid: 6m6DCQMASEM — "10 Millionaires Asked Me")

To a caller's Google/Meta agency charging about 10% of spend. Raise it up front: whenever you bring this in-house, we move to consulting until your team beats our numbers, and if it never does, we stay. Fees drop to about 3% of spend, or 20-30% of the old fee. It extends LTV, aligns incentives, and shifts you from servant to teacher.

### Only the number changed: 50% price rise on an acquired company (vid: 6m6DCQMASEM — "10 Millionaires Asked Me")

After a large analysis of a company bought a year earlier, his whole growth plan was doubling price. Same script, same reps, new number at the end. Nine calls with the founder settled it at +50%; he says they then closed a higher share since it had been mispriced. To a dentist asking if he could justify a rise: you need no reason.

### Brand is what lets B2C raise price (vid: EUW3rMp-Uvg — "Past $30M mistake")

Without a brand you're just arbitraging media. With one, a 10% to 20% price bump that holds demand can multiply the profit of a thin-margin consumer business. A brand is what people say about you when you're not there, so the path is keeping or beating your promises.

### Full and still not profitable means fix the unit (vid: F84olnKkseM — "Business Was Hard, 4 Concepts")

Owners at capacity with no money reach for a new location or product line. Test the smallest unit (one rep with 10 accounts, one therapist with 50 patients): if it doesn't pay at capacity, the model is flawed, so cut delivery cost or charge more. Don't expand first. A related example on imposter feelings: an agency owner who reached 150 clients but crashed on ops can sell his client-getting system by saying exactly that.

### Charge by wallet, but change one small thing (vid: Lc8DNduiwKA — "13 Ways to DESTROY Your Competition")

Selling the same thing at two prices bothers him, so he adds a small extra for the bigger wallet, such as a free cocktail with the pricier ticket. The offers now differ, so you can answer honestly if two buyers compare. Works especially well in services, and pricing is far quicker to pull than most competitive levers.

### Downsell guardrails: only the disqualified see it (vid: MwhsM7qijy8 — "Increase LTV Without More Customers")

Extends the cannibalization warning. Set written requirements for the core offer. Meet them and you never see the downsell; miss them and you get a low or no-touch DIY version that lets you ascend later. Reps then have a black and white rule rather than guessing at budget, and the paid-for lead still offsets CAC. Measure core offer take rate before and after adding it.

### Four market positions (vid: ONV__y1z7MI — "How The World's RICHEST Man Made His Money")

Bargain: cheapest, poor experience. Best value: Target over Walmart. Premium: Lexus, BMW. Luxury: no price cap, because the known cost is part of the value and status attaches to it. Raising price normally cuts volume; in luxury he says it can raise it. His case for this is Bernard Arnault, who built the top fortune selling to about 1% of people. Do not mock the $50K jacket; to that buyer it is a rounding error, so they just want the best.

### What a higher price buys your customer (vid: ONV__y1z7MI — "How The World's RICHEST Man Made His Money")

Extends emotional investment. Less demanding buyers: a $50 purchase can be half someone's bank balance, so they interrogate it, while the high-ticket client just asks for wire details. More to deliver with: a $5 cost sold at $8 leaves $3 to run the business, sold at $50 leaves $45 for service and talent. Then it compounds into testimonials, while low price compounds into chargebacks and refunds.

### Copy pricing from mature big companies, not your peers (vid: Q2VUuUGfpNY — "6 Hacks To Make More Money")

Most owners average the local market, but the market average is mostly broke. Do not take dental advice from someone with bad teeth. Look at established companies past their growth phase, whose price has already been tested and paid for continued growth. Caution: skip funded players buying share with low prices.

### Raise prospect quality and you raise the product (vid: Gh9zWsP8JpI — "Reversing your pricing")

Same service, abler person, better results. A higher bar improves the deliverable without changing it. Counterintuitive, and why price is more than a margin decision.

### Four prices, same revenue: take the lower one (vid: TooAB8Ow6cQ — "3 Hours of Money Making Advice")

A portfolio company tested a high-ticket upsell at $50K, $35K, $25K and $15K, and revenue came out identical. Provided margin holds, he'd take the lower price: about four times the customers to keep, resell, and earn word of mouth from. Separately, the Skool team notes high-ticket price often doesn't move conversion much because the logic of a big outcome holds, and only some people can't afford the gap, while a low-ticket buyer alone at the button reacts strongly.

### Three genie wishes: why price beats customers and retention (vid: _ArQlwPvGUA — "Magic Business Genie")

100 customers at $100 a month, $8K cost, $2K profit. Double customers: $4K profit. Double retention: same top line eventually, more profit because of less marketing and onboarding per dollar kept. Double price: $12K profit, 6x from one change, and even losing a third of buyers leaves you well ahead. People split test pages, scripts and offers but forget price, the strongest lever.

### Price raise rollout with a Van Westendorp survey (vid: _ArQlwPvGUA — "Magic Business Genie")

1) Survey customers: why they bought, income, job, source. Then the four questions (too expensive, too cheap, a stretch, a bargain), plus which value-stack item they would keep if only one remained and which they would never miss. 2) Tell the team first. 3) Tell customers past, present, future: how we got here, why now, what it funds. 4) Hit the list with urgency to clear the pipeline. 5) Expect a sales dip because you pulled buyers forward, let it settle, then read the math. Result he cites: researching the market, adding 50% and changing nothing else helped double profit of a $5M business in under eight weeks.

### Brand flywheel (vid: VPre_XMgKjs — "MrBeast $100 Billion")

Extends the commodity price gap. A small premium makes a spread, the spread gets reinvested in branding and innovation, the stronger brand supports a bigger premium, and around it goes. Hormozi's read on why LVMH's owner became the richest man: he owns pricing power on commodity goods.

### Cutting price to answer a competitor: about $5M a year (vid: tdLLGKoBojo — "I Lost $100 Million")

His 2019 reaction to a rival taking top gyms: add everything they offered, including heavy accountability groups, and drop from $1,000 to $800 a week for everyone. First comment: you could have charged me this all along. At 40% margins a 20% cut loses half the profit, before added delivery, and the exit multiple applied to it. Every price change draws complaints. Lesson: watch customers, not competitors.
