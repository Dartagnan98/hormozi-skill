## THE 4-PART INVESTMENT LENS

Evaluate every deal through four dimensions:

### 1. Yield
- How much cash flow comes in monthly?
- Hard money lending example: 4%/month = $100K/month on $2.5M = 48-96% annualized

### 2. Capital Preservation
- How likely is my money coming back?
- Startup = probably not getting money back
- Real estate with 2x collateral and personal guarantees = high preservation

### 3. Tax Advantage
- Will I get taxed at income or capital gains rates?
- Hard money lending = regular income (bad) -- 50% tax rate
- Equity appreciation = tax-free until sold (good)

### 4. Equity Growth
- Does the thing I'm buying become more valuable over time?
- Zero participation in upside = bad deal
- Owning equity in a growing business = compounding wealth

### Bad Deal Example (Wealth Advisor Pitch)
- $450K to a startup, 10% return at end of 12 months ($45K)
- Capital preservation: startup = probably not getting money back
- Yield: 10% (below S&P at ~20% that year)
- Tax: treated as income, effective return after taxes = 5.5%
- Equity growth: zero participation in upside
- Verdict: "Borderline unethical"

### Good Deal Example (Bridge Loan / Hard Money)
- $2.5M note to secure building out of foreclosure
- Building worth $5.1-5.6M (4 offers already, top buyer had $300K earnest money down)
- Personal guarantees from both buyers (net worths >$2.5M each)
- Yield: 4%/month = $100K/month. Doubles to 8% after month 4
- Capital preservation: building worth 2x+ the loan, personal guarantees, multiple backup buyers, first-position title lien
- Minimum carry: 2 months = $200K guaranteed even if deal closes in 24 hours
- Tax: treated as income (bad), but 27% after-tax return still makes sense
- Closed in 100+ days, total return: ~$350K (check back for $2.85M)
- After taxes (50% regular income): ~$175K net profit

## BUSINESS EVALUATION CRITERIA

### Green Flags (What Makes Hormozi Say Yes)

**Revenue Quality:**
- Recurring revenue (8-15x EBITDA multiple vs 4x for transactional)
- High gross margins (80%+ for service, 90%+ ideal)
- Net negative churn (referral % > churn % = infinite organic growth)
- LTV:CAC ratio of 3:1+ (automated) to 12:1+ (people-driven)

**Operational Quality:**
- Business can run without the founder (team-run, not owner-dependent)
- Three superstars in place: Acquisition, Delivery, Operations
- Multiple acquisition channels (not dependent on one platform)
- Clean unit economics at the location/unit level

**Market Quality:**
- Large TAM (total addressable market)
- Favorable supply/demand economics (growing demand outpacing new entrants)
- "The bloodier the water, the more fish are there" -- red oceans have biggest rewards
- Body-based services resist AI disruption (nail salons, dentists, haircuts, personal training)

**Founder Quality:**
- Coachable, willing to follow proven playbooks
- Not spread across multiple businesses
- Has proven the model works at small scale
- Character > cleverness: "You only get one name. You only get one reputation."

### Red Flags (What Makes Hormozi Say No)

**Revenue Problems:**
- Transactional revenue only (no recurring component)
- Below 80% gross margin
- High churn (>9% monthly in service businesses)
- Single acquisition channel dependency
- Revenue declining while owner claims to be working hard

**Operational Problems:**
- Owner IS the business (no one would buy it because it's just the owner with helpers)
- Trophy business: "Business is a trophy, not a vehicle for wealth" ($1.7M revenue, 6.4% margins)
- 500-item menu syndrome (trying to do too much, can't excel at anything)
- "Is your goal status or is your goal money?"

**Market Problems:**
- Shrinking TAM
- Too niche without room to expand
- Government regulatory risk (cannabis seed business example)
- Business model dependent on a single platform that could change rules

**Founder Problems:**
- Won't raise prices despite 60-80% close rates
- Multiple businesses, none profitable
- "I need a partner" when they really need to focus
- Entrepreneurs (vs intrapreneurs) -- "either they lose their juju or they take your business with them"

### Qualification Criteria (Gym Launch Example)
After implementing qualification filters (25+ clients, signed lease, full-time, ideally 1+ employee):
- Sales dropped from 100+/month to 40-50/month (cut in half)
- Escalations dropped from 22 to ZERO in first 4 weeks
- Qualified customers paid MORE than unqualified ones
- "Recipe for a higher profit business, happier customers, and better reputation"
- "Dear God, why did this take us three years?"

## DEAL STRUCTURES HORMOZI USES

### 1. Equity Investment (Primary Model)
- Take minority or majority equity stake
- Provide operational support, playbooks, pricing strategy
- Example: Enchanted Fairies -- killed the agency model, owned all locations directly. Founder made LESS for 9 months. Month 10: matched. 30 months: $2.5M/month with 30+ locations

### 2. Hard Money Lending
- Short-term, high-interest bridge loans secured by real assets
- $2.5M at 4%/month ($100K/month), doubling to 8% after month 4
- Defense layers: personal guarantees, first-position lien, multiple backup buyers, earnest money deposits
- Returns: 48-96% annualized yield
- Tax treatment: regular income (not capital gains) -- 50% tax rate
- "Trust is the most valuable thing you can have"

### 3. Licensing / Royalties
- Royalties come off the TOP (revenue share > profit share)
- "Somebody can play around with their profit. Top line is topline."
- Gym Launch was essentially a licensing model -- sold the system, nearly 100% margin
- Revenue share always better than profit share for the licensor

### 4. Majority Sale to Private Equity
- Sold 66% of Gym Launch + Prestige Labs for $46.2M to American Pacific Group (December 2021)
- Still owns 30%+ of Gym Launch
- Pre-COVID valuation was ~$150M
- Gym Launch grew 40% after Alex and Leila exited majority share under CEO Kale

### 5. Rollup Strategy (Prescribed to Others)
- Dentist association: bundle 10 practices/year, sell each bundle for $100M, keep 20%, give 80% to dentists
- Then sell the "rollup machine" itself at 10x its $20-30M/year profit = $200-300M exit
- vs. original one-time $25M exit
- PE firm with $35M portfolio: go vertical (same type = rollup). Buy 10 things for $1M each, sell all 10 together for $50M

### 6. Seller Financing / No-Money-Down Acquisitions
- Find businesses doing $250K+/year profit where owner is tired
- Agree on price first, THEN negotiate terms
- Get seller financing for 75% of the deal
- Friend combined four ~$1M/year info businesses: "Give it to me, I'll pay you the income for the next 2 years." Owned the $5M asset for nothing out of pocket

### 7. Profit Sharing + Equity Buy-In (Billionaire's Model)
- CEO gets 5% of profits + opportunity to buy increasing equity chunks over time
- One CEO started at 5-10% ownership, now owns ~30% of the largest company
- "Not being greedy grows the pie faster than keeping a bigger slice"

### 8. Insurance / Franchisor Model
- Get paid before delivering, may never need to deliver
- Predates the tax code. Oldest companies (180-300+ years) are insurance companies
- Franchisors: "get paid and then remit the remainder" -- control the money flow

## EBITDA MULTIPLES BY BUSINESS TYPE

### Transactional Businesses
- $10M revenue, $3M EBITDA = **4x multiple** = $12M enterprise value
- One-time purchases, no recurring component
- "Transactional business that hits $5M? Just worth the income"

### Recurring Revenue Businesses
- $10M revenue, $3M EBITDA = **8-15x multiple** = $24M-$45M enterprise value
- Netflix (fixed membership) vs Coca-Cola (habitual purchasing) -- both count as recurring/reoccurring

### Owner-Dependent vs Team-Run
- Owner-dependent $5M business = worth the income only (nobody would buy it)
- Team-run $5M business ($2M take-home, doesn't require owner) = $10-20M asset
- "You become wealthy from the things you own, not the things you do"

### Impact of Growth on Enterprise Value
- Going from $2M to $3M annual EBITDA = adding $10M+ to net worth (at 10x multiple)
- At S&P-level multiples (40x): $2M EBITDA increase = $80M net worth increase
- "What you build builds your net worth, not what you make"

### Specific Examples
- Gym Launch: sold at implied ~$70M total valuation (66% for $46.2M)
- Panda Express: $3.7B sales, 27% net margins, $935M distributions, 100% owner-operated (no franchises)
- Enchanted Fairies: grew to $2.5M/month ($30M/year run rate) in 30 months from agency pivot
- Dr. John Berardi / Precision Nutrition: 9-figure+ exit

## ENTERPRISE VALUE FRAMEWORKS

### What Drives Enterprise Value
Enterprise value = discount applied to future cash flows = how much money x how likely it will occur
- Lots of likely future money = big multiple
- Little uncertain money = small/no multiple

### How to Increase Enterprise Value
1. **Switch from transactional to recurring** (doubles or triples the multiple)
2. **Remove owner dependency** (makes the business sellable at all)
3. **Add multiple acquisition channels** (reduces risk, increases multiple)
4. **Improve gross margins** (80% -> 90% = 2x more profitable, 90% -> 95% = 2x again)
5. **Reduce churn** (10% -> 3% monthly = 3.3x LTV improvement)
6. **Build brand** (pricing power = Warren Buffett's "single most important decision in evaluating a business")

### The Continuity Premium
- Transactional $10M/$3M EBITDA = 4x = $12M
- Recurring $10M/$3M EBITDA = 8-15x = $24-45M
- Same revenue, same profit, 2-4x difference in what you can sell it for

### Billing Optimization for Enterprise Value
- Bill every 4 weeks (not monthly) = 13 cycles/year vs 12 = 8.3% more revenue
- For 20% profit business: ~41% increase in profit from this alone
- Add "3% processing fee" = straight to bottom line. For 10% profit business = 30% profit increase
- Get 2 forms of payment (card + ACH). ACH links to bank, rarely changes

## WHEN TO SELL VS HOLD

### Hormozi's Default: Hold
- "The wealthiest people in the world buy and build. Most times they don't sell."
- Warren Buffett model: See's Candy for 30+ years, Coca-Cola, GEICO
- Don't sell your business for an exit -- take loans against it
- $1M business grows to $2M: take $700K loan = tax-free cash, still own the business

### Reasons to Sell
- When a buyer values it significantly higher than you can grow it (American Pacific Group offered during market peak)
- When you've lost the fire for that specific business
- When a rollup opportunity makes the parts worth more as a whole
- When you need liquidity for a bigger play

### The Tax Argument for Holding
- 94% of Forbes 100 growth came from appreciation of equities, not income
- Selling triggers capital gains tax; holding lets appreciation compound tax-free
- Loans against appreciated assets = spendable money without triggering taxes
- "We're all taxed at 100% -- we don't get to keep it no matter what" (death)

### The "Don't Sell" Math
- Business at $10M EBITDA, 5x multiple = $50M valuation
- After-tax dividends at 37% rate: $6.3M/year
- Grow to $12M EBITDA: valuation goes to $60M, dividends $7.6M
- Net worth increase: $17.6M, effective tax rate only 21% (56% was tax-free appreciation)
- Selling would net far less after earnouts, transition periods, and taxes

### The Billionaire's Approach to Holding
- Man doing $1B+/year, 50 privately held companies, mid-70s
- "I don't believe businesses should make a profit. Profits are unnatural."
- How $100M -> $1B: "We gave up. Broke company into 100 pieces, gave equity to superstars, checked out. Five years later: $1 billion."
- Investment approach: multi-family real estate, never below 8 cap rate
- "More concerned with return OF capital than return ON capital"

## INVESTMENT PHILOSOPHY AND RULES

### Rule 1: Invest in Skills Before Markets
- Don't invest in stocks/real estate until money is flowing in FASTER than you can spend on skill acquisition
- $25K, $50K, $250K, $500K in the bank? Spend it ALL on acquiring skills
- Your business (where you have expertise) will almost always outperform passive investments
- "Somebody who has fewer skills needs more money"
- $1,000 in S&P = $1,100 at year end (10% return). $1,000 in sales training = $40K-$220K income increase = 18,000% return, every year going forward

### Rule 2: Framework Over Outcome
- Ray Dalio: "If I were to use the decision-making framework that would have made that deal a winner, I'd have to apply that same framework to all other situations that would have been losers, and my net net would cause me to lose"
- Applied to bitcoin: Buffett framework says don't buy speculative assets. Bitcoin went up. Still considers the decision correct
- "Don't change your swing every time you strike out"

### Rule 3: Wealth Comes from Appreciation, Not Income
- 94% of Forbes 100 growth = appreciation of equities, not income
- At 40x multiples: $2M EBITDA increase = $80M net worth increase
- Richest people live in highest-tax states (CA, NY) because they understand this
- "What you build builds your net worth, not what you make"

### Rule 4: Risk Management
- Jack Butcher: "You make fortunes by taking a lot of risk with a little bit of money. You maintain fortunes by taking small amounts of risk with a lot of money"
- Bezos: "Given a 10% chance of a 100x payoff, you should take that bet every time"
- Lynch: "A stock can only go to zero. But it can go infinitely high"
- "Baseball has truncated outcomes (max 4 runs). In business, you can score a thousand runs."
- Never bet the farm: "Don't put everything on black when you cannot afford to lose"

### Rule 5: Volatility Is Not Risk
- Insurance company example: loses money 1 out of 9 years (catastrophe years), makes a lot the other 8
- "My insurance business is not risky. It is volatile. Those are not the same things."
- Salespeople have volatile income but are LOW risk employees (they generate revenue)
- "If you want to decrease the volatility in your life, decrease your expenses to your salary"

### Rule 6: Wealth Is a Ratio
- "Wealth is a ratio between how much you spend versus how much you make"
- Salesperson making $140K living on $25K = 6x income-to-expense ratio
- Saving $115K/year at 10% returns = could technically retire in 2 years
- "A salesperson making $200K/year owns a million-dollar business. It's the same thing." (at 20% net margins)

### Rule 7: The Opportunity Cost Test
- Every dollar invested in the stock market is a dollar NOT invested in your skills/business
- Be "petrified" of investing in areas where people with deeper talent stacks will crush you
- Peter Lynch compounded at 30% for 15+ years -- but he was the BEST stock picker in history
- For most people: reinvest in what you're already world-class at

## BUSINESS SELECTION CRITERIA ("What Business to Start")

### The Perfect Business Model: UESA
- **U**nique -- Big competitive moat
- **E**xpensive -- Sells for a dollar, costs a penny (99% gross margin ideal)
- **S**ticky -- Habit-forming, high LTV
- **A**ir -- Digital/weightless, costs nothing to deliver
- Buffett: "Costs a penny, sells for a buck, habit-forming"

### The Three Ps
1. **Pain** -- Something you overcame personally
2. **Profession** -- What you do/did for work
3. **Passion** -- What you can't get enough of

### Opportunity Vehicle Assessment (Richard Schwarzman's Framework)
Three factors for pursuing any opportunity:
1. Really big potential (TAM)
2. Can be run by 10/10 talent
3. Makes economic sense (unit economics work)

### Four Business Shapes
1. **E-commerce**: Fast growth, hits breaking points (cash, traffic, distribution). "No gigantic businesses are drop shipping."
2. **Service**: Linear growth tied to headcount. Best for starting out.
3. **Software/SaaS**: Slow start, exponential once product-market fit achieved. Highest ceiling.
4. **Content/Media**: Slowest start. Years to build. Once working, ~100% margin.
- Buffett: "It's not as important how hard you row but what boat you are in"

### Business Growth Stage Benchmarks
- **$0-$1M**: One product, one channel. Reliable customer acquisition.
- **$1M-$10M**: Multi-products (cross-sells, upsells), sometimes multi-channel. 4-5x LTV.
- **$10M-$30M**: Professionalize (HR, legal, IT, accounting, compliance). You become a target.
- **$30M-$100M+**: Hub-and-spoke. Central ops with profit centers. Each run by an intrapreneur.

### Leverage Career Progression (Hormozi's Personal Path)
- Employee: 4 figures/month
- Self-employed: 5 figures/month
- Employer (gyms + turnarounds): 6 figures/month
- Licensing (content leverage): 7 figures/month
- Acquisition.com (capital + collaboration + content): 8 figures/month

### The $100M Blueprint
1. Get impossibly good at ONE thing (until people talk about you before you enter the room)
2. Monetize teaching that skill (high margin)
3. Reinvest profits into scalable asset (software, product, franchise)
4. Grow and exit (or hold forever)

### Body Services Are AI-Proof
- "Start a boring service business around the body" if starting from zero
- Cocoa Fit tried automated personal training machines -- went out of business
- "People hire trainers for human connection and accountability"
- Nail salons, dentists, haircuts, personal training: "People value the human exchange"

## TAX STRATEGY FOR INVESTORS

### The Big Rocks (What Actually Matters)
1. **Where you live**: Zero-tax state vs California (15-16%). Puerto Rico = 4% federal (6+ months)
2. **How you buy**: Buy $100 asset for $30 = instant 3x gain, untaxed. Compounds tax-free
3. **How/if you sell**: Don't sell. Hold forever. Buffett model.
4. **Live off loans**: Loans aren't income. Borrow against growth. As long as loans < growth = compound tax-free forever

### Tax Shelters Are Usually Not Worth It
- Conservation easements, captive insurance = "BBs" -- tiny impact
- IRS is 3-5 years behind. Most loopholes get unwound. Then fines + interest + legal
- Charlie Munger: "95% of tax solutions are swampland"
- Friend traveled 104 days/year for Puerto Rico tax residency: "Could you make 30% more if you didn't travel 104 days?" He hadn't thought about it
- Another friend missed their baby's birth for tax residency days
- Close relative got audited over captive insurance structure
- Hormozi spent $1.6M in legal/accounting fees, paid $22.68M in taxes over 5 years
- Could have saved ~$6M with an IDGT (intentionally defective grantor trust) but chose not to

### Seven Tax Beliefs
1. Attention is #1 most valuable asset. Never trade it for minor tax savings
2. Pay zero attention to tax savings schemes. Focus on making, not saving
3. Orders of magnitude thinking (10x, not save 10%)
4. Never dwell on what you would have saved on roads not taken
5. We're ALL taxed at 100% when we die -- chips stay on the table
6. Money exists to buy options. Don't trade optionality for money
7. "I'd prefer to be known as the person who paid his dues and still killed it"

### The 85-Year-Old Self Test for Tax/Investment Decisions
- "If I'm 85 years old, a decabillionaire, would that person care about saving 30% on taxes?"
- "Will I care if I have $9 billion or $11 billion when I die? No."
- "Don't sacrifice anything for me. I'm old. You're young. Enjoy."

## THE MONEY LADDER (Who Gets Paid When)

Restructure your business to be higher on the ladder:

1. **Employee**: Work first, paid 2-4 weeks later (lowest leverage)
2. **Self-employed**: Split (half now, half on delivery)
3. **Professional (doctor/surgeon)**: Paid first, then work
4. **Banks**: Preferred creditor, paid first if things go wrong
5. **Insurance**: Paid the whole time, may never deliver
6. **God/Franchisors**: 10% off the top, forever (highest leverage)

"You will be compensated in proportion to the risk you're willing to take."
"The people who do this well have it be very risky for other people and not risky at all for them."

### Four paths to mega money (vid: sL16tsGafcQ — "4 paths to wealth")

Your money + your business = bootstrap. Others' money + your business = raise. Your money + others' businesses = invest. Others' + others' = fund management. His tally of the top 11 on Forbes: mostly raised capital, a few bootstrapped (Ballmer's Microsoft, Dell, Waltons), Buffett investing. Fund managers show up a few places lower.

### Bootstrap costs every debt except money (vid: sL16tsGafcQ — "4 paths to wealth")

Start with ignorance debt paid on your own dime, not friends' money. But you take on management, technical, and data debts money would've solved. It's like building the car factory inside the car, so it's the slowest. It keeps control and equity and limits what you can pursue (no global robotics).

### Raising: two customers and real risks (vid: sL16tsGafcQ — "4 paths to wealth")

Good for huge upfront costs or winner-take-all. Prices out rivals, buys top talent, lets you lose on acquisition. Costs: serving investors too, dilution, liquidation prefs and ratchets (his partner's first exit paid less than the headline), board seats (Jobs). VC is grand slam money; you're n of 1, so a failure takes a decade of your life.

### Investing: slow, chill, and built on active income (vid: sL16tsGafcQ — "4 paths to wealth")

His most relaxed period was running just a family office. Almost nobody gets rich this way first. Buffett bought stock as a child and made most wealth from 80 to 95. Real estate creates the most main street millionaires, not billionaires. Winners concentrate, 5 to 8 bets with real edge.

### Fund math at $5M in (vid: sL16tsGafcQ — "4 paths to wealth")

GP $5M + LP $95M + $200M debt buys $300M of companies. 20% annually for 6 years is about 2.98x, or $900M. Pay debt and interest ($300M), LP capital ($95M), pref ($40M); $465M is left to split per terms. Even 10-20% is $46-90M for the GP. Costs: years-long feedback, many masters, rich on paper.

### Capital follows deals (vid: sL16tsGafcQ — "4 paths to wealth")

A mentor told him there's no lack of capital, only of good deals. Bring a believable shot at 5x with six risks mitigated, and the investor asks how much you need. Proprietary deal flow and a singular thesis earn better splits. Example: a black walnut tree fund he was pitched, cash flowing annually from year three.

### How ACQ underwrites hypotheticals (vid: Uzr0gv4N4-s — "Change My Mind, Win $10,000")

A big-brand future is a hypothetical with execution risk, so investors discount: 10% odds on $1B, and the check gets written that way. Trailing profit gets underwritten because the future's unknown. Tons of gross profit with a clear return thesis is fine; thin margins called reinvestment are not.

### How the finalists got judged (vid: eq1bnI0JoXU — "8 entrepreneurs compete for $100K")

Online delivery and technical skill were pros for one; strong LTV:CAC, reoccurring revenue, and surviving COVID when legally shut for the other, against lower margin and less cash flow. Watch what they do over what they say. A mid opportunity needs violent execution to be worth the investment.

### Expansion: the fifth trait next to Unique Expensive Sticky Air (vid: 3fsJFUvA6Ts — "What Makes The Perfect Business")

Add a market that is not shrinking, and grade all five as dials, not yes or no. If you can generate demand you do not need a tailwind, you only need to avoid a headwind. His examples: formal education down about 6% a year versus alternative education up over 20% a year, the bet behind School. Priority if you can only pick one: sticky. If you have none, fix retention first; if you are early in your career and the industry has no retention, switching may be the smart move.

### Air defined: count the variables to add one unit (vid: 3fsJFUvA6Ts — "What Makes The Perfect Business")

Operational complexity is how many things you must actively manage to expand production. A podcast ad read is sell, read, post at any size. A 100-restaurant chain is staff, suppliers, spoiling inventory, buildouts, leases, parking and permits for even a small extra unit. Low complexity plus low capex means less dilution and faster growth without outside money.

### Capex can be the moat (the exception to Air) (vid: 3fsJFUvA6Ts — "What Makes The Perfect Business")

Against everyone digging by hand, buying a shovel puts you ahead. Once he has some success, Hormozi likes businesses that cost capital to expand, because fewer people can afford to enter, which brings pricing power. Weak test case: social media agencies can be sticky and high margin, yet nearly anyone can start one, so price falls. Capital is defensible only so far; things only you know how to do rank higher.

### Cannot raise money? Your deal is bad, not your pitch (vid: 3fsJFUvA6Ts — "What Makes The Perfect Business")

Great return on invested capital makes you a magnet for money. Hormozi's illustration for a franchise: $100K to open with a 3-year payback is mediocre; $100K with $300K back on average in year one sells itself. Most owners who cannot raise have weak core economics, not a missing skill. Buying growth with outside money only makes sense when share has real advantages like network effects, which is rare; School is his example.

### Cheap moats: brand and the patent test (vid: 3fsJFUvA6Ts — "What Makes The Perfect Business")

Without capital, your moat is recipes, processes, patents or brand. Patent test has three parts: new, non-obvious, useful, so list what only you do that passes all three (you still have to defend it). Brand turns a commodity unique: he says mass-market makeup and the drugstore's own label can roll off the same belt with different stamps, yet the name earns a premium and higher conversion and retention. Coke is his example with both recipe and brand.

### Burn it down when the next thing is orders of magnitude better (vid: 0EqJD2o-Mnk — "Helping 6 Business Owners Scale")

Caller with an $11M construction firm plus a fast recurring elevator company. Reasonable advice: hire leaders. Hormozi's advice: exit construction in 90 days at any price. He fire-sold six gyms in 90 days, made about what one should have fetched, and within six months did about $1M a month on the next thing. He only thinks in opportunity cost: if a quarter of focus adds recurring revenue worth more than a perfect exit, stop preparing the sale. Also keep passive passive; active work on real estate for 5% better returns costs more than it earns.

### Three fallacies of copying Warren Buffett (vid: 6x3re5-Ms1E — "Ultra Rich Playbook")

Passive-investing fans miss three things. Temperament: he bought his first stock at seven, weeks after Pearl Harbor. Era: his career rode the biggest growth run in American history. Luck: the single top person has every required trait plus luck. Plenty of brilliant investors stopped far lower or had fewer years. Hormozi's points: income funds investing, so build that first; and obscene money gets made in a new way, so the top skill is reaching your own conclusion and acting against the crowd. Opportunities read as risk until the moment they close.

### C corp only pays if you redeploy (vid: FNJpJG-sSXM — "Biden tax plan")

At the proposed rates he discusses (28% corporate, about 40% on dividends at the top), pulling money out of a C corp is taxed twice. It makes sense when earnings stay in and fund M&A or capex that lift the company's value. Small businesses rarely have that, so LLCs usually fit better. Have your accountant run both side by side, then get back to building the business, the real source of wealth.

### Tax on unrealized gains, the math of a paper win (vid: FNJpJG-sSXM — "Biden tax plan")

His 2021 objection, using a proposal he doubted would pass: $100M net worth grows 15% while income is $2M. A 20% minimum tax on the $15M gain is $3M, more than was earned, and spreading over 9 years only stacks each new year on top. Founders would sell or borrow to pay on gains that may vanish. His own unpopular alternative: slash income and capital gains tax and take a near-total death tax, so builders give wealth away while living.

### Treasuries as the bank account (vid: GkL2KDOf2NM — "Recession Advice")

Depositors are creditors of the bank. In 2022 banks paid him about 0.1% to 1% while earning about 4% lending to the government, and above FDIC limits a failure can trap you. So he holds idle cash in US treasuries: less likely to fail than a bank, pays more, and he says he could borrow up to about 80% against them while still earning interest. He treats it as a checking account, not an investment. This is the "cash equivalents" of Buffett headlines.

### Portfolio follows your knowledge (vid: GkL2KDOf2NM — "Recession Advice")

A friend's stocks-plus-real-estate barbell left him making about five investments in two years, roughly 10% of his cash, because it wasn't his game. Then he saw Dave Ramsey ask Graham Stefan what share of his knowledge was real estate: about 80%, so 80% in real estate was correctly balanced. Billionaires do few things, ones they understand, many times. Hormozi's order: invest in yourself, then indexes, then large checks only inside your main game (service businesses, where he offered one founder an open check).

### Billionaires' math: own it, then work backwards (vid: LMlbWtUFa4E — "How Billionaires Make Their Billions")

Extends the multiple idea with after-tax contrast: at 10x, going from $5M to $6M profit adds only about $500K take-home yet $10M of untaxed asset value. Back-solve the goal: about $70M EBITDA at the 15x he cites for companies that size is $1B, or roughly $1.4M profit a week. Then at a 30% margin he rounds to about $300M top line.

### Why a buyer pays 10x: the leveraged example (vid: LMlbWtUFa4E — "How Billionaires Make Their Billions")

His illustration: buy a $50M business earning $5M, with $15M down and the rest borrowed. Grow profit 20% a year: about $12.5M by year five, with $40M-plus earned along the way, and now worth about $125M at the same 10x. Institutional buyers think this way, so build your asset to fit that thinking.

### Company value = customers x lifetime gross profit / risk, and the 10-tile buyer checklist (vid: HKbFUWJwEG0 — "Reverse Engineered the Perfect Business")

Only three levers set what an investor pays: more customers, more lifetime gross profit per customer, less risk that it continues. Cutting the bottom of the fraction counts as much as growing the top. His Value Acceleration Method names 10 pieces a buyer checks: leadership runs the day-to-day, marketing without the founder, delivery without the founder, multiple reliable acquisition channels, reliable recurring revenue, diverse customers, automated metrics, high cash flow plus profit plus growth plus a story tied to a trend, audit-ready financials, and $5M+ EBITDA. Find the missing tiles and fill those; the end picture is always the same.

### Acquisition.com portfolio averages and the $5M EBITDA line (vid: HKbFUWJwEG0 — "Reverse Engineered the Perfect Business")

His team's printout: after 12 months, revenue 1.8x and profit 3.01x on average; after 24 months, revenue 2.8x and profit 4.7x. He says under $10M revenue expect about 1 to 4x earnings. Institutions mostly won't buy below $5M EBITDA, partly since a real management team usually exists by then and partly since helping grow a $1M business takes as much effort as a $20M one. He told founders years of effort don't make a business valuable; buyers price the future without you.

### The 13 moats (vid: Lc8DNduiwKA — "13 Ways to DESTROY Your Competition")

His list of ways only one seller can sell: 1) network effects (user base or closed product ecosystem), 2) exclusive resource, data or tech, 3) government grants and licences, 4) economies of scale, 5) vertical integration, 6) brand and loyalty, his favourite because it takes skill and time rather than capital, 7) strategic pricing, 8) exclusive contracts (supply, customers, distribution), 9) huge capital requirements, 10) IP, 11) mergers and acquisitions, 12) innovative business model, 13) owned distribution. One done fully is enough. He quotes Dan Kennedy on scale pricing: being second cheapest gets you nothing.

### His Skool bet: several tailwinds stacked, not one silver bullet (vid: IFpHasZ7jN4 — "54.1% First Dollar Online")

Munger's lollapalooza applied to his largest investment. The checks: a big shift (he sizes US formal education near $850B, shrinking about 6% a year, while online learning grows about 20%, so roughly the same dollars cross over each year); his own history in that money; mission fit with his audience; golden ratio above 1, meaning it grows without him; and model leverage. Same $10M revenue and $3M profit, double it: a service firm at 5x profit adds about $15M of value, e-commerce at 2x revenue adds $20M, software at 10 to 20x revenue adds $100M or more. Same work, more for it. Six months of talks came first, because anything he endorses that sucks lands on him.

### If-then investing rules (vid: INm4U2S7Vu8 — "24 Investing Rules")

Principles as fixed if-then calls, so a past decision does the thinking. Size: if you can't buy it twice, don't buy once; his largest allocation might be 10%. Grand slam feel: get a second opinion. Can't name how you lose: wait. Feels rushed or FOMO: slow down. One deal on the table: find others, since one looks better with nothing to compare. No diligence, no model showing the math, or a slow responder: no deal. Too complicated: pass. Costs sleep: don't buy. No track record, or a partner you'd dread: no deal. Doesn't build your reputation: pass, no neutral moves. Something keeps working: do more of it. Always under-borrow: anything times zero is zero. Partner disagrees: don't move, even with final authority. Worried about a thing: insure it (annuities cover running out of money).

### Golden retriever and the GP seat (vid: INm4U2S7Vu8 — "24 Investing Rules")

Starts each pitch: pretend I'm a golden retriever who speaks no English. Define terms, draw it, see examples, and draw it back or you don't get it; no upside to seeming smart. At $10M to $30M investable, demand a share of the general partner, so you sit with whoever actually runs it. Buying to sell is trading, not investing, and mixing the rules for business and investing is where people get hurt.

### Big returns on a slice cover the whole pile (vid: QQGHCG8d1So — "Emailed myself my failures")

A 10x on one tenth of your money doubles the total, so outsized returns on a small share can beat inflation for everything. That's why he caps real estate, where he has no edge, and concentrates on internet businesses, where he does, buying with a margin of safety.

### Grant Cardone through the leverage lens (vid: QQGHCG8d1So — "Emailed myself my failures")

Hormozi's four C's: collaboration, capital, code, content. Grade people on which they use; Facebook has all four. Cardone has three (no code), and Hormozi predicted his next move would be a bank, because getting people to save is easier than getting them to invest, and deposits can be levered to buy more. A prediction, not a reported fact. He says to turn envy of someone ahead of you into curiosity about how they do it.

### A charity that funds itself (vid: QQGHCG8d1So — "Emailed myself my failures")

He sees solicited charity as broken: no value exchange, always dependent on goodwill. His alternative for Acquisition.com: a for-profit that attracts top talent and commits a fixed share of profit to causes every year, so giving grows with the company. You then only need to convince one owner to give, not everyone.

### Only two things you can do with money (vid: aO56wn5sBSA — "A billionaire on my money")

Per his mentor, who sold for a reported $3B: buy stuff, or lend it for interest. Lending risk falls with three things: top of the cap stack, transparency, liquidity. Then rate any investment on four: yield, appreciation, tax advantage, preservation of capital.

### Buckets for an operator: the risk stays in the business (vid: aO56wn5sBSA — "A billionaire on my money")

Cash from the companies goes to indexes first (about half), then multifamily, then cash and speculation. It's preservation, because net worth comes from the businesses, where he has expertise. Indexes win partly because money deploys at once; real estate sits idle while you hunt for deals. Multifamily because of real-estate-professional depreciation, not because it's exciting. Get tax advice; speech-to-text, and rates and rules may be dated.

### Keep cash visible and deals come to you (vid: aO56wn5sBSA — "A billionaire on my money")

He says he gets short notes, like lending about $2.5M for roughly 90 days at a few hundred thousand, because people know he has cash. Dry powder is an opportunity source as well as peace of mind. Figures are rough. Pair with his rule that mistakes love a rushed decision.

### Look for bets you can't lose: the nickel story (vid: Tu6YDG0AZ5k — "Billion Dollar Decision")

A story he credits to Tony Robbins, secondhand: a billionaire stores nickels because the metal is worth more than the coin, so when the coin is withdrawn he melts them for about 40%, and meanwhile it stays worth face value. The point is the shape: high return, low risk, downside close to none. Wealthy people hunt for bets that pay either way.

### Capital allocation by what your brain knows (vid: ULGT0Qpglek — "17 Life-Changing Conversations")

Deal flow stops being the problem at wealth; decision fatigue, diligence and lawyer calls take over. He overheard an advisor ask a real estate investor: draw a circle, how much of your brain knows real estate? About 85%. Stocks, 10 to 15%. That is your allocation. Hormozi's version: he knows business, so he invests only in businesses, can write a $5M to $10M check on one and comfortably, and turns everything else down at once.

### Sell or keep: the delta math (vid: _gcqwupsza8 — "I Sold My Company at 31")

Selling isn't more wealth at first: value already in equity becomes cash minus tax. The real test is whether what the cash grows into, plus what you build with freed focus, beats what the sold stake would be worth in five years. He rejected debt-funded M&A on the keep side because a mistake could undo everything; in a friend's phrase, don't bet the empire for a pot of gold. Banker numbers need a discount: he says they inflate to get you signed, and he heard $150M, then about $45M, then sold at a $46.2M price for two-thirds.

### Taking your face off before a sale, and selling off the bottom (vid: _gcqwupsza8 — "I Sold My Company at 31")

Over 12 months he quit product meetings, client calls and his own events, and promoted gym owners as the brand's face. What remained: a weekly CEO one-on-one, a 90-minute exec call, quarterly meetups. Bankers advised pausing when quarterly profit dropped from $3M to $4M to about $500K. He kept going; two firms bid at the bottom, then a sharp rebound drew two more.

### Goose vs eggs: find what's actually sellable (vid: VxKwz6hBVZU — "Build a Valuable Company")

The goose makes value; the eggs are the things a buyer pays for. Most owners try to sell the goose, which is usually valuable but unsellable. His own: Acquisition.com is the goose, the portfolio companies are eggs. Teaching businesses are the common trap: a coach of event venues, Amazon stores or accounting firms has key man risk, no stickiness, and volatile acquisition. The venues, stores and firms themselves are faceless and already trade.

### Run the how-to business as a rollup pipeline (vid: VxKwz6hBVZU — "Build a Valuable Company")

If you teach people a sellable business, you do a rollup's work up front. Standardize their systems, colors and prices from day one, then offer your top 10% a place in a group sale. His sketch: roll up 20 to 50 every other year while keeping the goose that brings people in. Why they'd pay you: investing has a volume premium, the reverse of Costco, because big money pays up to do one deal rather than 100. His example: a firm worth 4x earnings alone, 12x in a group, keeps double even after giving up 30%.

### Screen for sellability: dependence, stickiness, deal activity (vid: VxKwz6hBVZU — "Build a Valuable Company")

Ask of the business, each service line, and your customers' businesses: does it need you, and is any revenue sticky? Then search M&A activity for that type of business. None found means no market. Some found: compare those businesses with yours and close the gap. Treat the company as a product and the acquirer as the customer. Micro gyms rarely trade because they have key man risk too; accounting firms trade constantly because clients don't switch.

### Bigger than the company: sell a minority, not the whole (vid: VxKwz6hBVZU — "Build a Valuable Company")

Gym Launch sold because it was bigger than him then: he was out of delivery and ads, with a leadership team, recurring revenue and several channels. If you outgrow the company you stay key man risk, so nobody buys all of it. Your option is a minority sale or raise at the holdco, as he says MrBeast did and he could do at Acquisition.com, to take chips off or fund a purchase. That's a liquidity event, not an exit.

### TAM only counts on a 3-to-5-year view (vid: aOq1Hdz0w3k — "What I look for to invest")

Extends the opportunity vehicle check. Facebook's early market looked like college students. Founders grow in vision, adjacent silos open up, and M&A buys in more market, so today's TAM isn't the long-term one. That is no license to add avatars early: one can probably carry you to about $100M a year. Failing example: wireless has huge demand and pennies per added user but fails on competing with Verizon and AT&T, two boxes of three.

### Keep passive passive (vid: jqo0lVveh98 — "If You Have a Service Business")

To a roofer with real estate on the side. Owning it is no distraction unless it eats your days or you start planning gazebos and new roofs for it. Making passive money active costs time to gain a few percent, while active income returns far more. Hence his preference for REITs and funds with good partners.

### How a $100M sale becomes $40M (vid: okA9Yt2KZuk — "How I Scaled My Sales Team")

His illustrative walk: seller financing, an earnout and rolled equity leave about $60M at close. A broker at about 4%, staff bonuses and escrow take it to about $50M. 20% US capital gains leaves about $40M. Then the questions: what can $40M buy that yields the $15M a year you gave up, and will the buyer's asks of your time differ from now? His own answer: nothing in his life would change, so build it sellable, then keep it and borrow against it.

### Deal killers to clear before institutional buyers look (vid: pWbSl7d0tEc — "I sold 8 businesses by age 32")

About $5M is the smallest deal real institutional money looks at. What kills or discounts a sale: too small; a few big customers, worse if the relationship is yours; no repeatable acquisition process; talking to too few buyers; thinking it's worth more than it is; no growth or small TAM (buyers extrapolate a tiny dip to infinity, and a tiny gain too, so sustain 12-18 months of growth first); key man risk; one channel. Selling too late beats everyone: "I'll be ready in 18 months" is the standard answer. His definition: enterprise value is all future sales, discounted by the risk they don't happen.

### Hiring and paying the banker (vid: pWbSl7d0tEc — "I sold 8 businesses by age 32")

Under about $5M use a broker; above, a banker with recent deals in your market and at your size, since their buyers write checks scaled to their funds. Get in by networking, send a one-page teaser, and meet four to six. Negotiate out upfront and breakup fees and set a minimum below which they earn nothing. Pay them on cash you receive, not enterprise value, or a 10% sale at a $1B value pays them on the full billion. Write yourself a letter with your number and reasons before they inflate it. Bankers want a deal done, like real estate agents, more than your best deal, so push them to chase. His final buyer came from a follow-up his CFO made them do, not the first set.

### The sale funnel and calendar (vid: pWbSl7d0tEc — "I sold 8 businesses by age 32")

His rough funnel: about 500 buyers contacted, some sign NDAs, about half of those engaged get management presentations, two to four LOIs, bid against each other, one deal. Talking to 10 people and concluding no one wants it is a volume error. Calendar: about 12 weeks to pick bankers, 20 weeks of preparing and marketing, 60-90 days of diligence. Plan 12-18 months from start to check, with a strong team running things meanwhile. In diligence they interview current and past customers and employees and background-check management; his buyer spent about $1M on it. Non-compliance blew up two nine-figure deals for an acquaintance.

### Speed is a tactic against you (vid: pWbSl7d0tEc — "I sold 8 businesses by age 32")

Every buyer sold him fast closing as worth a lower price. One promised 30 days, took 112, and he had accepted worse terms for it. Later: take your time, the company is growing. Long processes favor buyers, so the only real way to speed one up is keep growing hard, because the valuation moves and you can break away for other offers.

### Institutions move only on "if I don't get this, the deal is off" (vid: pWbSl7d0tEc — "I sold 8 businesses by age 32")

His observation: they say no to everything and call it their structure until you say you'd walk on that point. So win before starting by having options and a business you don't want to sell. Then ruthlessly tell bankers what matters most. His own Gym Launch deal: sold 66%, real rolled equity, and said upfront no seller financing, no earnout, no transition or consulting. Reasoning: if they want two more years of me, I might as well keep it. Price and terms are separate; a headline $100M with paper terms can land near $17M.

### Purchase agreement traps (vid: pWbSl7d0tEc — "I sold 8 businesses by age 32")

Accept warranties that you own what you sell; fight indemnities beyond your control. Non-compete: know your next business and write it in plainly; honesty negotiates easier than guessing. Rolled equity trap: the new company's governing documents may bar holders from competing, so the non-compete lasts as long as you hold. Anything you used to sell the deal but left out of the schedules counts as if unsaid. Definitions can be worth more than clauses; redefining one word in his non-compete changed the document. Working capital arrives last: argue for as little as the business needs, and dividend out idle cash beforehand so it doesn't get counted as needed. Keep escrow under about 10%, released in phases.

### Strategic beats financial on price, not on supply (vid: pWbSl7d0tEc — "I sold 8 businesses by age 32")

A strategic (a bigger car wash chain buying yours) already has the systems, so makes more from your business and pays a better multiple. There are more financial buyers, so that's the trade. Family offices buy cash flow and are price-driven. Hormozi's tiebreak: many sellers regret the top bid over a 10% lower one with better people, and your team has to work with them.

### Price a new channel at the exit, not at the ad account (vid: rj7rzOdj84A — "Your Business Is NOT What You Think It Is")

His illustration: $2M EBITDA at 5x is $10M. A second acquisition channel can double profit and also lowers a buyer's risk, so two multipliers: at least $10M more value, maybe $15M. Reframe the loss: losing $100K over six months figuring out TikTok ads is really a $100K, six-month investment for $10M. Most owners quit, calling it "ads don't work."

### Investing is saying no faster (vid: rj7rzOdj84A — "Your Business Is NOT What You Think It Is")

He thought it was many deals at good prices. Of 22 deals over about two and a half years, four made 90% of returns, and many were a waste of time and money. Growing the largest company, about $100M a year, by $50M can take less work than getting a small one going, and bigger profit sells at a higher multiple. It's easier to grow bigger, and you get paid more for it.

### Before signing an LOI: next day plan, and will your day change (vid: rc7cxL7ql7Y — "58 Mins of Advice")

Extends the sale decision. Founders he knows split in two: exit with no plan and hit a crisis of meaning, or know what's next and get right back to work; he started Acquisition.com the day after selling Gym Launch. Test the terms: if your daily behavior stays the same after signing, why sell? One-time revenue with no retention, as a caller had, argues for taking a clean all-cash offer, since it could vanish with an ad account.

### Roll-up integration is the world's longest checklist (vid: rc7cxL7ql7Y — "58 Mins of Advice")

Answer to a caller doing about $2M in dog waste removal who wants to buy competitors. Split each target into components: routes and customer list, field staff, admin. You're buying the book of business, not their marketing. Write an SOP per component, then grow one checklist with every integration until it covers everything and runs faster. ### FOMO means go slow (vid: rp1PzCxj3eU — "42 Recession Proof Money Hacks")

Mistakes love a rush. Getting in is always easier than getting out, so put all the friction on the buy and all the ease on the sell. FOMO only shows up because your time horizon is too short: when you feel it, pause and put space between you and the decision.

### Ask how long to double, not what's the IRR (vid: rp1PzCxj3eU — "42 Recession Proof Money Hacks")

"Will this grow 25% a year" is hard to answer. "How long to double, how long to triple" is easier and finds better answers. Standard matters too: he says his billionaire friends look for 50%-plus annualized, not the S&P's 10%. Don't assume low risk and great returns can't coexist.

### Diversify vertically: where you sit on the capital stack (vid: rp1PzCxj3eU — "42 Recession Proof Money Hacks")

People diversify across asset classes and forget the other axis: position in line. Banks rarely lose because they are preferred creditors, paid first. When the tide goes out, seniority decides who gets money back. Also: diversification is a hedge against ignorance, per Buffett, so play one game to its natural conclusion rather than a little of everything.

### House money, a known exit, and a starter deal (vid: rp1PzCxj3eU — "42 Recession Proof Money Hacks")

Recoup principal first, then get aggressive with money you can't lose. Before investing, make them walk you through each exact step of getting your money back; a vague answer tells you what you need to know. With a friend's Airbnb idea or anything else that looks great on paper, make the first deal small to see how they actually behave.

### Returns are in the terms; never take the standard deal (vid: rp1PzCxj3eU — "42 Recession Proof Money Hacks")

He can offer anyone a billion for their business on his terms and never lose, which shows that price means little alone. Name the terms that concern you and ask what can be done. There is always a better version, and you learn the options only through many conversations. Leverage comes from not needing the deal: he has walked away and had people return days later.

### Three questions for a too-good deal: the $50K Ohio house (vid: tdLLGKoBojo — "I Lost $100 Million")

A seller pitched 25%-plus cash on cash at $50K a house. Buying just one saved him: the third bedroom was a closet, power was run illegally from the neighbor, the driveway needed about $7,000, tenants went to a hotel, and the seller had paid about $19K. His billion-dollar-fund friend's test: if it's so good, why sell? Now he asks how long they've held (under 2 or 3 years, ignore), then buys alongside them.

### Losses grow with distance from your core (vid: tdLLGKoBojo — "I Lost $100 Million")

He passed on $1M of ethereum near $100, then FOMOed into crypto near the peak and lost $500K in weeks. Survivable because the bet was small against monthly income. His big money always came from marketing and sales arbitrage he was present for, such as $10 customers worth $1,000, not investments. A mentor's line: if you know how to create wealth, just do that.

### Margin loans: the mechanics and the call math (vid: yr1DrcPCKEg — "Ultra wealthy loans")

Extends live off loans. Brokers lend roughly 30-65% of a portfolio, varying by size and history; he quotes 1-2% interest as of 2022, which moves with rates. No closing costs or fees; you pay interest only while holding it. Loan proceeds aren't income, which is the wealthy's tax play. The risk: borrow $60K on $100K, the portfolio falls to $50K, and at a 60% limit you may only owe $30K, so repay $30K now or they sell your assets at the bottom. Margin calls hit when assets are worth least, and that forced selling feeds crashes.

### His margin rule: short, secured, and far under the limit (vid: yr1DrcPCKEg — "Ultra wealthy loans")

Borrow 10-20% tops, so a call can't realistically happen. He used margin only for 6-12 month deals, such as hard money lending at 12-20% against an asset he could seize, funded at 1-2%. At filming he carried zero debt, no mortgage. His logic, building on Dave Ramsey's point that risk over a long enough time horizon exposes you to zero: doubling his money changes nothing in his life, losing it changes everything, so don't risk what you need for what you don't.
