# Firing Framework
## Source: Alex Hormozi (602 video transcripts)

## Hormozi's Firing Philosophy

- "We fire people like crazy, but people don't quit"
- "Low performers don't last"
- High performance culture = winning team that self-selects
- If someone new doesn't assimilate to the culture, the team rejects them
- "Gym Launch is a dangerous place to work -- if you are a winner, you will love it here"

## The Partner Who Fired Late People

- Locked the door at meeting time, fired everyone who arrived late
- "He reset the bar of this is how we perform"
- Set the tone for the entire culture

## 3-Week Rule (for trained frontline employees)

- "If you have trained someone properly and they are still below expectations three weeks in, cut them"
- Keeps culture high, shows team you respect the standard
- "No one feels like anyone is freeloading"
- Entry-level advertising jobs aren't complex -- they require traits (grit, endurance)
- Meta-skill of grit takes longer to train than the skill itself
- If below expectations after 3 weeks of proper training: cut them

## The Vince Story

- Hormozi worked at Smoothie King, trained by owner Dave to do everything right
- Manager "Vince" told him "we don't do any of that stuff"
- "Do you have a Vince? Someone secretly destroying your business?"
- Managers who lower the bar behind the owner's back are the biggest threat

## When to Fire Partners

- See partnerships reference for full framework
- If business isn't making money: both walk away
- Have the conversation directly: "You said you'd do sales, you're not doing sales. What do you think is fair?"

### Your worst person is your bar (vid: H8QfAfCbOXs — "If I could start over, culture")

Culture is defined horizontally, per function, not by comparing frontline staff to executives. The worst person on a team is what you signed off on as acceptable. When he lets mediocre people go, the team is relieved. Move before they have to tell you, and they'll trust your judgment.

### The mediocre middle is the real clog (vid: H8QfAfCbOXs — "If I could start over, culture")

True losers get removed because they're painful. The limit is the clock-in, clock-out middle whose slow work gets redone by people who care. Nothing demotivates a winner more than being forced to work with a loser. Give a few chances, not nine.

### Make them brace before you deliver bad news (vid: MPHn-nW30QU — "14 Years of Good Advice")

Doctors are taught to prepare families before telling them; do the same for firings and breakups. Open with: this isn't going to be a fun conversation and I'm not looking forward to it, but we have to have it.
Bracing lowers the pain they feel and the chance of a wild reaction. It also lets out your own nerves, so you can get through the actual message.

### The breakeven test for letting people go (vid: R7McLmx73Xw — "30 lives in 30 days, what he learned")

Stuck on removing two weak people? Imagine the business were breaking even or losing money. You would act without guilt. The action and their outcome are identical, so the only thing that changed is the story you tell yourself.
Keeping 2 of 10 who drag the team sacrifices the other 8 to dodge two hard talks, and it risks a forced layoff later. Cut from strength, ahead of need.
Advanced owners are not right more often. They just act faster once someone is wrong. On notice: warn about the behavior and its consequences, but do not announce a firing unless the law requires it.

### Personal slump: grace scaled to tenure, with a clock (vid: QgmRlzh0Y7M — "How To Play To Win")

Treat tenure as a credit balance. Someone with 3 or 4 good years gets real slack. Say it plainly: you have this many months, and I need you back after that.
The question to track: is this a misstep or a new behavior pattern? Once lateness and disengagement compound and the team notices, your job is protecting the team.
Keep the record of accommodations so you can cite them. Firing a long-timer out of the blue tells everyone no performance earns slack. Listing thing one, two, three over 4 months reads as reasonable. Saving them also avoids rehiring and lost knowledge.

### Fire Susan: the real costs and the fake talk (vid: h6y0nYVZgwE — "Not Unbelievably Rich Yet")

What you see is her best behavior, and customers will tell you stories once she's gone. She sets your bar, repels good hires and eats the owner's attention, even in bed at night. Most owners go home and hold a "communication" talk instead because they fear for her. Say you care about her and also about everyone else here, so she has to go. Fear of the gap: the 40 hours you pay her for are often about 5 hours when you do it, and you come out able to retrain the job. Leila's framing: you're great, I'm a bad manager, either way it's not working and I can't fire me.

### Applause on the way out (vid: oK2_u-uS_Bo — "Money Lessons I Learned In Marriage")

Separate the role from the human. When someone you've invested in fits better elsewhere, even at a competitor, Leila tells them to take it, where his old instinct was hostile. Graceful exits came back in every form for them: referred customers, training the replacement, recruiting, later deals. Those doors shut if you put heads on spikes.
