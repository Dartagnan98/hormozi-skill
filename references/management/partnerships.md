# Partnership Frameworks
## Source: Alex Hormozi (602 video transcripts)

## Only Three Reasons to Have a Partner

1. They have skills you do not have
2. They have money you don't have
3. They have time you don't have
- "If both of you are 'cool' and love the same thing, one of you is unnecessary"

## Structure Recommendations

- Prefer performance-based relationships over equity partnerships
- Use phantom equity with profit/revenue sharing instead of real equity
- Standard arrangement: percentage of revenue or profit based on performance
- Example: e-commerce companies that give 10% of top-line from attributed marketing

## Talk About the Ugly First

- "Right now you guys are in lust"
- Define: exit strategy, roles and responsibilities, what happens if someone leaves
- "Have the alimony conversation before the marriage"

## How to Exit a Partnership

- If business isn't making money: both walk away
- Two pieces to resolve: equity (who owns what) and debt (who owes what)
- Transfer equity via simple document, create promissory note for debt
- "I've done million dollar loans on one page"
- Promissory note: borrower, lender, no interest, amount, date, payment increments, default terms
- Have the conversation directly: "You said you'd do sales, you're not doing sales. What do you think is fair?"
- "The biggest hack in all relationships: the way you talk about someone behind their back is what you should tell them"

## Hormozi's Partnership Failures

- Had 8 failed partnerships
- Early career: took on partners left and right because he didn't know what he was doing
- "I was so stingy with equity early on... then swung back to not wanting to give anyone equity... now fallen in the middle path"

## PARTNERSHIP DETAILS (Deep Pass)

### Three Pillars of Every Business
1. **Acquisition** (rainmaker -- marketing and sales)
2. **Product** (fulfillment -- what you deliver)
3. **Operations** (everything else -- payroll, taxes, legal, IT, finance, HR)
- If both partners like the same things, one is unnecessary
- Operators can be hired for less; rainmakers and product people are more valuable

### Nine Failed Partnerships
- Had 9 failed partnerships, nearly all concurrent
- Made all money in life POST-partnerships (after stopping 4+ years ago)
- Most failed because both partners liked the same things (both acquisition-oriented)
- No contracts, no terms, no agreements -- "afraid of messing up the vibe"
- Biggest wealth dilution: giving away 50% equity on day one before the business grows
- "Most expensive equity is given in the beginning"

### Billionaire's Three Superstars Rule
- Private call with man doing $1B+/year revenue, 50 privately held companies
- Three superstars per business: Acquisition, Delivery, Operations
- "If you have three superstars, that business can grow on its own"
- "I've never NOT thought someone was a superstar and later realized they were"

### Selecting Trusted Advisors (Two-Trait Framework)
1. **Integrity:** Do they have your best interest at heart? Test by aligning incentives
2. **Efficacy:** Do they have the skill? Test by interviewing as many as possible
3. If forced to choose: 8/10 efficacy + trusted intention beats 10/10 efficacy + uncertain intention
4. Red flags: new entities (spinning up fresh LLCs), principal been indicted, can't explain simply
5. "Explain it to a golden retriever who speaks Spanish"

### Partner disagreements are information gaps (vid: R7McLmx73Xw — "30 lives in 30 days, what he learned")

His rule with Layla: no agreement, no move. That does not mean compromise. They stay in the disagreement until both hold all the same facts, because with identical information they tend to reach very similar decisions. So the first thought in any split is: what do you know that I do not?
This needs long shared values. Without them, let the partner with the better track record make the hard calls.
Before a hard talk, say out loud that the critique is about the idea, not the person, and that you are on the same team.

### Getting equity in a family business: make them name the price (vid: WeH2E1fGgaw — "Daily Q&A: selling, comp and failure")

Scaling your parents' company but own none of it? Ask the real question out loud: what would I have to do to own half someday, or all of it? Then they state a price, for example 10% a year for 10 years.
Now it is a calculation: could you build something larger in that same time without their permission? If yes, prove it. If no, stop complaining.
His general frame for hard talks: tell the complete truth. Say to them what you would say behind their back, minus the insults, stick to facts.

### Insult vs criticism for the hard partner talk (vid: 6m6DCQMASEM — "10 Millionaires Asked Me")

An insult attacks character. Criticism only names the gap between what was stated and what happened: you own sales, we have no new sales. Caller's agency had two whales near 90% of revenue, and it landed one fast when forced to, so ability was not the issue. His read: one partner is content with current income, and an unspoken split on growth turns into resentment. Say what you want, own your part for not being clear, and offer: change the activity, or we work on separate things.

### Obsessive, not compulsive, and obsessed with something else (vid: IFpHasZ7jN4 — "54.1% First Dollar Online")

Best entrepreneur profile: hold attention on one goal for years without compulsively changing direction. Pick a partner as obsessed as you about a different thing, or one of you is redundant. Three functions must be covered: product, promotion, people. Equity need not be thirds, and one can be an exceptional employee.

### Defend in the contract, then live as if it doesn't exist (vid: INm4U2S7Vu8 — "24 Investing Rules")

Negotiate terms as if they are all that matters, walking through what happens if each side fails, then deliver week to week as if a year-long client could leave next month. Defensive in contracting, offensive in delivery. If someone says they only do handshakes, walk: no one who said that to him wasn't shady.

### Partner must bring knowledge, money, or time you lack (vid: JShQ8BX08rs — "10 KEYS to a TERRIBLE Partnership")

Munger inversion: design the worst partnership and do the opposite. Same experience, same time, same money means one of you is unneeded and both gave up half. A need doesn't mean equity: you pay an accountant, you don't partner with one. Ask if you could simply pay for it. Equity is the priciest thing you give, so reserve it for those who will drive growth over 10 years.

### Write expectations to force the hard talk (vid: JShQ8BX08rs — "10 KEYS to a TERRIBLE Partnership")

Contracts are not about lawyers; they make you have the talks you'd avoid, and if you can't have them with no money at stake, you won't later. Write it with the hope of never rereading it. Make it fair up front: taking too much, or doing too much for too little, grows resentment and later renegotiation. Think ahead and give the other side more.

### Align on mission, values, lifestyle: the same-data test (vid: JShQ8BX08rs — "10 KEYS to a TERRIBLE Partnership")

Mission is where to go, values how to get there, lifestyle how they live. A saver and a partner in debt collide, since a personal mess always gets dragged into the business. Be proud, not just okay, to introduce them. Litmus: given the same data, would you both decide the same? Complementary skills with aligned mission, values and interests is the target.

### Allan: don't steamroll the person who has to run it (vid: SasEJE4FI-I — "Your Failures Can Make You Rich")

The only real fight he says he and Leila ever had was about starting Allan. She said there was no bandwidth; he told her to think bigger and overrode her. It was software, new to him, while CEO of other companies, and an existing business suffered as she warned. The concept was sound, the error was launching without the operator on board, wife or not. In game terms: a co-op where one player doesn't want this game.

### If you don't agree, don't move forward (vid: ULGT0Qpglek — "17 Life-Changing Conversations")

Marriage advice from a pastor that he and Leila also run in business. Almost nothing is truly urgent, so a disagreement just means slow down. First question each time: what information are you using? Often one has data the other lacks. With shared values and mission they tend to land on the same decision, and he credits this with saving many mistakes.

### Yes partner, no partner: turn the no into a checklist (vid: aBPWCdaJJqA — "I Helped 6 Business Owners")

Good partnerships pair a yes person with a no person; alone, he'd have 100 businesses and Leila none. The no usually comes from whoever bears the day-to-day pain. Don't argue speed versus proof. Ask what they'd need to see to feel fine doing the next one, agree it, and reuse the same checklist for location three.

### Be fast to extend trust, faster to take it back (vid: oK2_u-uS_Bo — "Money Lessons I Learned In Marriage")

He was quick to trust outsiders and slow with his own team; Leila is the reverse: walls for outsiders, full trust once you're in, removed if you give reason. Outside the team, believe people's colours the first time. If someone has a history, she doesn't want to be their come-up story: there are enough people who have done well all along. Let redemption happen on someone else's dime; he says most of his second chances burned him. When exiting partnerships: do right by every stakeholder, even if it means writing check after check, since screwing one is what you regret.

### Influencer equity: upfront, performance, time (vid: oRqnTOT9ZG8 — "Monetize An Audience in 2026")

The creator's public association is permanent the moment it's announced, so take some equity up front no matter what. Tie a share to performance (extra customers, up to a cap, for example up to 10%) and a share to time (his sample: 25% up front, 25% on performance, the rest vesting over four years of promotion). Define the work in the deal, not "I'll promote": link in bio one week a month, one long form mention, five short posts or stories, ads recorded monthly for whitelisting.

### Bad partnership vs bad partner (vid: tdLLGKoBojo — "I Lost $100 Million")

Bad partnership: fine people, wrong structure. He sold two-thirds of his growing gym for about what it cost to start, and whoever works in the business needs a salary on top of ownership. Bad partner: bad intent. One asked him to guarantee the lease, front the money and do the work; after a launch of 370 sales the account was drained, money went abroad and bankruptcy blocked a suit. He knew of a past fraud indictment. Believe people the first time.
