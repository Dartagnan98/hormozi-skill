## Source: Alex Hormozi (602 video transcripts)

## Department Grading System

- Apply the Talent Grid (What-How-Who) to each department: marketing, sales, product, shared services
- Grade yourself: "Where am I involved? What level am I at in each department?"
- Your STRENGTHS often become the bottleneck (you're comfortable delegating what you don't know)
- Goal: all departments at Level 3 so you can operate above as CEO/owner

### Key Insight: Strengths as Bottleneck

- "It's actually your strengths which almost always become the bottleneck in the business"
- You delegate weaknesses easily but hold onto strengths too long

## Training Framework (Document-Demonstrate-Duplicate)

### Three Steps

1. **Document**: Make a checklist of what you do
   - Pro tip: video yourself doing it, then make checklist from the recording
   - "You don't think about checklisting while doing it"
2. **Demonstrate**: Do it in front of them (they watch you)
3. **Duplicate**: They do it in front of you (you give feedback)
   - Cycle until they can give themselves feedback = mastered the skill

### Short Windows for Proving

- Entry-level advertising jobs aren't complex -- they require traits (grit, endurance)
- Meta-skill of grit takes longer to train than the skill itself
- If below expectations after 3 weeks of proper training: cut them

### Shadow Train Method (for leadership roles)

1. They watch you do it
2. They do it in front of you
3. Support their independence (available but not involved)
- Initially more ad hoc calls, over time decisions get automated down to them
- Full handoff = they own it completely

## Culture Building

### Smarts vs Hearts

- Most entrepreneurs overestimate smarts, underestimate hearts
- "Business strategy overarchingly is not that complex"
- Lost in execution, not in strategy
- Two companies with same strategy: the one with better culture wins EVERY time

### Championship Team Mentality

- Everyone showed up to practice, tried hardest, aligned
- Rules are the same for everyone -- execution separates winners
- Competitors have access to same talent pool and can see your strategy
- Difference: ability to execute and have people execute on your behalf

### John Wooden Principle

- "Advanced people never don't do the basics"
- Every winning gym/business: they follow up leads within 5 minutes, follow a clear sales framework, have good onboarding
- "Nothing they say is revolutionary. It's just that they actually do it."

### Culture at Scale

- "When you scale, what you scale is culture and training"
- Culture = telling people what to do and WHY
- Training = making sure they know HOW
- Strong core team self-selects: new hires either assimilate or get rejected
- "Having that culture allows you to spit people out quickly but also retain"

### The Bar-Setting Principle

- You are the most distilled version of the culture
- Your level of intolerance sets the ceiling
- Gets diluted at every level below you
- "If someone cares more about my customers than I do, they should rightfully run the company"

## Buyback Your Time (Control Framework)

### The Entrepreneurial Control Journey

1. Start: shift from lack of control (job/parents) to complete control
2. Problem: the complete control that got you here stifles growth (there's only one of you)
3. Must progressively relinquish control

### Sequence of Buying Back Time

1. Lower-skilled support tasks (assistant)
2. Higher-level admin (billing, contracts, collections)
3. Sales ("No one can sell like me" -- but you can't outsell 12 people)
4. Sales management (recruiting, hiring, training, compensating a sales team)
5. Leadership (too many departments to lead everyone directly)

### The Identity Issue

- Each level requires giving up part of your identity
- "I used to think I was such a great salesman"
- The thing you're holding on to IS the thing you need to give up

### Rule of Thumb: "Know Where the Bodies Are Buried"

- If you can't name problems in a department, you're too far away
- If you only hear good news from a department, you're too far away
- Need more transparency, not more control
- "Every department has issues -- it's a business made of humans"

### The Trust Paradox

- "How can you be free if you control everything?"
- You relinquish control but keep full accountability for results
- "I'm going to give this over to you, I'm losing control, and yet I'm still responsible"
- Trust issues from childhood = the reason most entrepreneurs can't scale

   - Helps you detach, gain perspective, realize other things matter

   - This is Hormozi + Leila's dynamic

- Hormozi + Leila actively create space (different offices, opposite sides of house, different meetings)
- "Familiarity, though comforting, is not attractive"

## Charlie Munger's 8 Laws (As Applied by Hormozi)

### 1. Invert, Always Invert

- From Johnny Carson: try to solve problems in reverse
- Start at what you DON'T want and solve from that direction
- Fresh perspective that forward-thinking doesn't give

### 2. Don't Ingest Chemicals to Alter Your State

- "I can't imagine anyone thinking 'my life is so much better because I do drugs and drink'"
- One of the principles Munger found consistent in successful people

### 3. Don't Be Envious

- Buffett and Munger: "We have never met anyone who's done well in life who was envious of other people"
- "The more money I make, the poorer I feel" -- measuring stick changes
- $100K felt wealthiest because measuring unit was still meals
- Now measuring unit is millions -- someone always has more
- "Simply an imaginary way to make yourself suffer"

### 4. Don't Resent People

- King who wrote names in a drawer, later read them to see how life dealt with those people
- "People who do bad things eventually get caught up to"
- Someone existing in that state is already suffering

### 5. Be Reliable

- "If you just get this one right, it's very difficult to be unsuccessful"
- Hormozi's high school rival story: kid wasn't athletic, wasn't quick, but showed up every time
- Did all extra credit, all homework, went to every teacher event
- Got into Duke (Hormozi went to Vanderbilt)
- "He was more reliable than I was. More long-suffering. More consistent."

### 6. Don't Overspend Your Income

- "Wealth is a ratio between income and expenses, not a number"
- If you live on 1% of income, you're infinitely wealthy (100 years of living saved in one year)
- Hormozi tries to live on 10% of income
- When he had $1M in bank, still lived on $30K/year (3%)
- "I felt so free because I had way more money than I would ever need"
- Two ways to increase wealth ratio: increase income OR decrease expenses

### 7. Don't Learn Only from Your Own Mistakes

- Learning sources evolve with stage:
  1. Starting out: trade TIME for mentorship (work for free for small business owner)
  2. Growing: buy courses/coaching
  3. Established: join masterminds (peers at your level)
  4. $8 figures+: networking + advisers (minority equity in exchange for teaching)
  5. All levels: heroes (consumed content from people you'll never meet)
- "I've been directly mentored by Charlie despite never meeting the man"

### 8. Don't Quit After 3-4 Failures

- "You will fail many times. If you quit early, you guarantee misery."
- Don't even count failures -- the number means nothing

### Hormozi's Addition: Don't Crack Under Pressure

**Three Tactics for Resilience:**

1. **Negative Visualization** (from Epictetus via Trevor)
   - Imagine the bad thing happening 1,000 times in a row
   - How would you feel on the 1,000th time? You wouldn't care.
   - "You can rapidly force acclamation to stress by visualizing it happening thousands of times"

2. **Expect the Worst Before Starting New Behaviors**
   - Going to work out? Expect to be tired, unmotivated, busy. EVERY time.
   - If that's your baseline, you're never surprised or derailed
   - Any positive deviation is a bonus

3. **Everything Becomes Fine Mist** (from Elon Musk)
   - Universe will expand until everything is a fine mist
   - Everything you create will eventually disappear
   - "Nothing matters, which means we get to choose what we create meaning with"
   - Our brains are "meaning making machines" -- no inherent meaning, we assign it
   - "That has been so calming and has relieved so much pressure for me mentally"

## System for Outlearning Everyone

### 5 Levels of Competence (Student Mindset)

| Level | Type | Belief |
|-------|------|--------|
| 1 | Positive Active | "No one has proven it can't be done. I will be the best student ever no matter what." |
| 2 | Positive Passive | "He did it, so I can do it." (Replicate before iterate) |
| 3 | Neutral Passive | "Let's see if it works for me." (Where most people sit) |
| 4 | Negative Passive | "It might work for other people, but probably won't work for me." (Half-asses) |
| 5 | Negative Active | "I can prove this won't work for me. I'm special. Nothing works for me." |

### Key Insight on Level 5

- Highest potential to flip to Level 1
- "They have the active -- they're just proving the wrong thing"
- Once they switch direction, they become absolute brand champions
- Ask them: "Do you want to be right or do you want [the outcome]?"

### Why Unequal Outcomes Exist (Bottom-Up)

- Number of skills going in predicts success
- Each education/program provides "bricks" building a bridge
- We attribute success to the last brick (most recent) when it was cumulative
- "Skills and education build on themselves"
- More meta-skills (reading, learning, grit) = better ability to bridge gaps where instructions are vague

### Replicate Before You Iterate

- "I will do everything they said to the tea to replicate what they've done"
- First get the same result, THEN iterate with your other skills
- "Unless you've learned to replicate, you don't know why it worked to begin with"

### ROI Every Experience

- Even terrible experiences teach what NOT to do (inversion)
- "You can always benefit from it or benefit from the inverse of it"
- "I will win whether it's with you, the next guy, or the guy after that"

### Morpheus/Yoda Principle

- "Stop trying to hit me and hit me" (The Matrix)
- "There is no try" (Star Wars)
- Replace: "Stop trying to make your marketing work and MAKE your marketing work"
- "Try" allows the possibility of failure -- remove it

## Leveling Up: Why We Get Stuck

### The Progression

1. **Skills** (early stage): Stack skills together to become proficient, fill the holes
2. **Amplification** (growth stage): Deepen skills through repetition ("willing to get punched in the face over and over")
3. **Character Traits** (scaling stage): Focus, determination, patience, saying no, persistence

### The Woman in the Red Dress (Matrix Analogy)

- Opportunities = seductive distractions
- The higher you go, the more attractive the distractions become
- At $2M/year, the temptation to start a supplement company
- At $10M/year, the temptation to start a software company
- "The more talented you are, the more opportunities you know you could crush, which just means more things you have to say no to"

### Grit (Angela Duckworth)

- "Stick-to-itness" -- how likely are you to abandon a path for another
- "You can be successful at anything you want, just not everything you want"

### The Fitness Analogy

- Minimum effective dose vs threshold for new adaptation
- Huge "no man's land" in between where you work enough to maintain but not enough to improve
- If income stayed same for years, your skill set stayed the same
- "If your skill set has stayed the same, it's usually because you lack the character traits to deepen those skills"

### The Nature of Change Changes

- Early: level up skills
- Later: level up character traits
- "The actual thing that you need to improve changes -- not just skills, but the nature of the change itself changes"
- "Sometimes the skill itself is to not do other things"

## CULTURE BUILDING FRAMEWORK (Batch 7 -- Expanded)

### Observable Culture Definition Process
1. Define culture into observable terms (not vague values)
2. Demonstrate it yourself (Gandhi sugar story)
3. Get them to do it, watch what they did
4. Immediately correct failures, publicly praise successes
5. Next stage: they start giving kudos to EACH OTHER
6. Final stage: they start correcting EACH OTHER in private ("Hey man, you're cutting corners. It's going to get us all in trouble")
- "Every business you think does an amazing job has standards. Every business you think is shit, the owner didn't have standards."

### Paired Metrics for Team Incentives (Specific Example)
- Speed incentive without quality metric = guaranteed corner-cutting
- Paired system example (lawn care):
  - Pay: performance-based per job (speed incentive)
  - Quality: complaint = redo on own time (zero pay for redo)
  - Escalation: 2-3 complaints in a month = lose bonus for month
  - Third offense: lose job
  - Documentation: video of every completed lawn

### Why Small Businesses Stay Small
- "The reason most businesses stay small is because they think small and they act small"
- "You have to have the beliefs before you get the outcomes of a bigger business"
- "If everyone's responsible for everything, no one's responsible for anything"
- First hire should always be strong operational second
- Time study: write down what you do every 15 minutes for a week to understand where time goes

## FROM $100M LOST CHAPTERS

### Maker or Manager (from Paul Graham, Applied by Hormozi)
- Paul Graham concept: there are only two modes of working -- making or managing
- **Makers** need long uninterrupted blocks (4+ hours) to produce their best work
- **Managers** work in 30-60 minute meeting blocks, context-switching all day
- A single meeting in the middle of a maker's day destroys the entire block
- "Time is the only resource. People who make the most money trade it for the highest return."

### The Wealth Equation
- **Overworked** (80 hrs/wk): $2M income, net worth = cash in bank
- **Wealthy** (10 hrs/wk): $2M income, net worth = cash + $10M in equity
- Same income, wildly different wealth
- "You get rich from what you make. You become wealthy from what you own."
- The transition from rich to wealthy requires building systems that run without you

### "Everything I Thought About Employees Was Wrong"
- Early Hormozi mindset: employees are a cost, minimize headcount, do everything yourself
- Reframe: employees are the mechanism through which you multiply your output
- The cold outreach bottleneck story: tried to scale by doing more outreach himself, hit a ceiling
- Solution: hired employees to do the Core Four on his behalf -- output multiplied
- "The way you scale is by getting other people to do the Core Four on your behalf"
- Employees aren't an expense -- they're an investment with measurable ROI (total payroll / engaged leads = cost per lead)

### Local vs global empathy (vid: H8QfAfCbOXs — "If I could start over, culture")

Keeping a weak person is empathy for one at the expense of everyone else. Take the local pain so the team gets the global benefit: I carry this guilt so the rest don't have to suffer. The same frame applies to founders who won't stop answering every customer personally.

### Public deadline, unified team (vid: 5rOIS7GHD5Y — "Personal brand in 2027")

Set a date, announce it, and make it hard to cancel. He says nothing beats the energy and alignment that gives a team. It's why Jobs and Musk set insane public timelines. Book launches and a company sale were his own belt moments.

### Who, what, when, then why not sooner (vid: ZTSI3DDP_4A — "Social strategy 2027, The Algorithm")

End every meeting with who does what by when. Then ask actual time on task: if it's 4 hours, what's more important than this today? Often nothing, so it's done by end of day. Moving from weekly to daily updates is a 7x increase in team speed. Call vendors hourly if you need to pull something forward.

### What would it take? (vid: fj5uxdv_j5Y — "97% of how to get rich")

Can-we questions get a no from someone lacking your context, deciding on your behalf. What-would-it-take assumes success and returns a price you can judge. Walk it down: would a billion do it? A million? Often it's one $100K hire to earn $1M.

### Trust means being punishable (vid: k5-57282taI — "How the top 1% think about trust")

Four types by who risks and who punishes. You give someone a secret, they could punish you. They give you one, you could punish them. You rely on someone's word or advice, and the environment punishes you. They rely on yours, and it punishes them. Name the type to explain why you don't trust someone.

### Zero punishment builds trust (vid: k5-57282taI — "How the top 1% think about trust")

A relationship's ceiling is the shared context between two people, and context is also ammunition. One betrayal undoes all prior cycles, like a tree cut at the root that still stands. React well when told something, never use it later, keep your word. Winning a short-term argument isn't worth it.

### Chasing compliance: talk as if most people already did it (vid: MPHn-nW30QU — "14 Years of Good Advice")

Don't announce that nobody filled out the survey. That tells people that not doing it is the safe, normal position.
Say instead: most of you have handled this, a few know who you are, get it in by end of day so we hit 100%. People want to stay with the herd, so now the holdout feels like the odd one out.

### Acquisition triangle: didn't know, don't know how, don't want to (vid: IMowPVgcWbA — "SIMPLE Managerial Framework")

Andy Grove said a boss's job is to train and motivate. Hormozi adds a third cause after a new sales manager skipped call reviews because no one had asked for them. 1) Expectation: say it, repeat it, write it down, make them report on it. 2) Training: how to do it, how you like it done, and how to fit it in their workload; do two together and record it. 3) Motivation: bonus, purpose, and personal payoff. Work the three in order and "I was busy" stops being an answer, since it may be training on priorities or plain lack of motive.

### Script from Leila: "It's not like you. Is it this, this or this?" (vid: IMowPVgcWbA — "SIMPLE Managerial Framework")

Open with "It's not like you to miss your end-of-week reports," which embeds a compliment and attacks the behavior, not the person. Then: did I not tell you, do you not know how, or do you not want to? Clear boxes make them answer honestly. If you fix one and it still fails, it's motivation, and you can have the real talk. Warning: moving a failing person to another open role is the tempting move and usually wrong. Ask if you'd hire them for it over the normal candidate.

### Give the why at three levels (vid: IMowPVgcWbA — "SIMPLE Managerial Framework")

Work without purpose feels like digging a hole to fill it back in. For CRM call notes: global (finance has better collection calls, onboarding is personal), then personal (notes screen out tire kickers so you close more of your calls). Tie it back to their own money whenever you can.

### Inverted version, plus values and pocketbook (vid: JDkiAxSd5Ms — "My Employees Don't Take My Business Serious")

Extends the Acquisition triangle. Add a values appeal: "You're the kind of person who is honest, and you wouldn't promise what we can't deliver; no notes means we can't fill your promise." Then the wallet: part of the bonus depends on notes on 95%+ of sales calls. A coworker they care about (Susie in finance working overtime to fill the gaps a five-minute note would cover) is the most human layer.

### Back the idea you doubt, unless it does real damage (vid: OpeN4O5myIg — "This Email Campaign Generates Sales")

The challenge came from the marketing director; Hormozi and the CEO saw a distraction. They let it run, and he credits it with over $100M in enterprise value. Reject every idea and your team stops having them. People nearer the work also hold data a CEO only sees in snippets. His test: will this hurt the brand, and is it a hill worth dying on?

### Micromanaging has only two causes (vid: QQGHCG8d1So — "Emailed myself my failures")

A friend holds the CEO title while the founder micromanages him. Hormozi's diagnosis: either you don't trust a capable person, or the person isn't capable and you hired badly. Both are your fault. Once the right person is in the seat, extend trust before it's earned, since trust tends to come back and the machine doesn't run without it. You make more that way even after getting burned sometimes.

### "My pleasure" only spread when the founder said it (vid: TIH1w-KuATk — "7 Secrets Behind Chick-fil-A")

Truett Cathy wanted staff to answer thanks with "my pleasure," and they studied hospitality and hired from Ritz-Carlton, yet it didn't take until he changed how he spoke himself. Hormozi's estimate: people learn 10x more from what you do than say. So pick only the few values you'd actually never break. A CEO who runs late can't preach punctuality.

### The incentive box: add or remove, good or bad (vid: WRW7eY6K4Jo — "Why Punishment Doesn't Work")

From talks with his friend Dr. Trevor Cashy. Four moves: add a good thing (cookie), remove a good thing (no games), add a bad thing (spanking), remove a bad thing (no dishes). Punishment makes people avoid the punishment, and that isn't the same as doing what you want: punish a kid for sneaking out and the lesson is don't get caught. Avoidance sprays in any direction away from the pain; a reward points at one outcome. So use the two that add a good or remove a bad. Nagging a spouse just teaches them to avoid being nagged.

### Reinforce the small steps, mostly with status (vid: WRW7eY6K4Jo — "Why Punishment Doesn't Work")

Break an activity into many incremental steps and reinforce each. The reward rarely needs to be money: a specific "that thing you did yesterday was great" works. He cites Jim Collins's research as he recalls it: performance falls when people hear only criticism and rises on mostly positive feedback. Skip guessing what's in someone's head. Measure the circumstance, the incentive and whether the outcome happened.

### Values that don't repel anyone are platitudes (vid: kW2vDMAmlPI — "How I know who to trust")

A value is a non-negotiable you already live, not an aspiration: you hire and fire on it. It has to pick a side (justice or mercy) and screen people out. Write it in words your team actually says: Gym Launch used "speed is king" and "do the boring work" rather than "work ethic". Three is his sweet spot, the number of lenses a brain can hold during a decision, and it is three out of three, not two. The point is scaling decisions: someone can judge a hire without him. Reading the list should give you the vibe of the place and tell you who wouldn't fit.

### A cookie when the plane lands (vid: h6y0nYVZgwE — "Not Unbelievably Rich Yet")

Most management is silence when things go well and a hammer when they crash. Like doing dishes for a spouse who never thanks you: people assume it doesn't matter and quietly stop, and you learn months later you've been paying for work not happening. Praise the routine on-time landing.

### Double time: half today's job, half tomorrow's (vid: h6y0nYVZgwE — "Not Unbelievably Rich Yet")

He spent half of each day on the job he has and half on the job he wants next, calling it moving at twice the speed, and it compounds. Same logic when you're stuck absorbing a fired employee's duties: you learn the role and fix its systems.

### Discretionary effort: the gap fear can't reach (vid: oK2_u-uS_Bo — "Money Lessons I Learned In Marriage")

Run on punishment and people do the minimum to avoid it, and start avoiding you, since you only talk to them when something is wrong. Everything between keeping the job and their best is discretionary. Leila's method: fast feedback in the project tool all day on small pieces of work, plus a form at hiring listing favourites, so praise comes with a gift tied to what they said they like. Her rule: never stifle a generous impulse, send it as soon as you think it. Read The Motive; he found he was the bad example. Even if your mission is to get rich, don't say that.

### If you're still upset tomorrow, chew their head off (vid: oK2_u-uS_Bo — "Money Lessons I Learned In Marriage")

Leila's rule for his anger. Nine times out of ten he woke up to: it wasn't malicious, just dumb. Then ask how to make it less likely next time. You never regret a night's sleep before responding, but rage feels powerful in the moment and leaves an anger hangover, and the anger then apology cycle is what abusive relationships look like.

### Reward trades speed for durability (vid: oK2_u-uS_Bo — "Money Lessons I Learned In Marriage")

Extends punishment fading and reward staying. Punishment changes behaviour fast, but it stops when you leave the room and you must keep raising it. Reward takes a longer on-ramp and more patience, then people stay self-sufficient when you're gone. Make it tactical by finding small reward loops: the person he most liked filming with nodded along behind the camera, giving live feedback. They broke that job into a handful of such activities, then trained and rewarded each.

### The feedback question: what would you tell me if I wouldn't get angry? (vid: oK2_u-uS_Bo — "Money Lessons I Learned In Marriage")

Leila's question for breaking a CEO bubble. Chew people out and they stop bringing bad news, and then the truth too, and decisions are only as good as their data. Answers run like: you're short, you're late to meetings, it takes you ages to reply. Expect your face to react early on, and ask again anyway. Good data makes you look like a genius.

### What to give up at each revenue band (vid: okA9Yt2KZuk — "How I Scaled My Sales Team")

Extends the buy-back-time sequence with his rough bands. Past $1M: give up fulfillment. $1M to $10M: stop selling, and learn to teach, recruit and manage sellers. $10M to $30M: give up marketing. $30M to $50M plus: give up leading, becoming capital and connections; the last thing to go is decisions. The levels mirror the org chart: do, manage, manage managers, lead leaders, advise. If stuck, ask what you're holding that must go.

### Nobody credits beating yourself up in silence (vid: p39nLmVtOjc — "You Made Your Customers ANGRY")

What he told the team member behind a costly error. Credit goes for two things only: making it right and making sure it never recurs. Skip guilt and shame. If you mess up, treat your boss as the customer in the angry boat: minimize it and they'll be the one to make you upset.

### How vague you can be tracks how skilled they are (vid: rc7cxL7ql7Y — "58 Mins of Advice")

In any role, directive vagueness is proportional to domain skill. He believes anyone can be taught anything; the question is whether that is the best return on your time compared to someone else. Scaling a service is scaling culture and training: culture by letting go of whoever lacks the attitude until only those who have it remain, training by teaching people to teach and to deal with people, down to how to greet someone, the way Chick-fil-A does.

### Management Diamond: a five-question hard talk (extends the reasons people don't do it) (vid: s6tkRztZwYc — "How to Articulate Your Thoughts")

Walk through it out loud, without blame: did you know I wanted this, how to do it, by when? Were you motivated? Was anything blocking you? Fix each one that's true. Deadline is its own cause, and blockers can be odd: his editors uploaded faster from home, so he paid to run Google Fiber into the office. Asking turns an accusation into problem solving.

### "Stop being a dick" is not a directive (extends observable instruction) (vid: s6tkRztZwYc — "How to Articulate Your Thoughts")

Soft skills are hard to teach only because the words bundle many behaviors. A strong employee ignored talks from four leaders. Hormozi got specific examples of what people complained about (such as how he opened calls), listed what to stop, and said I don't care if you hate everyone, just behave this way. Colleagues noticed a turnaround within a month. You manage behavior, not character.

### A checklist tells you who's wrong: them or you (vid: s6tkRztZwYc — "How to Articulate Your Thoughts")

Score recordings against the list. Sarah does 3 of 11 items and Caroline 9 of 11, with higher scores: that's a clear coaching talk instead of "why does Sarah suck." If someone does all 11 and still scores low, the checklist is wrong and that's on you. Soft-skill items such as checking your background on an onboarding call count too.

### Add feedback loops to slow jobs; motivate from deprivation (vid: s6tkRztZwYc — "How to Articulate Your Thoughts")

Sales and editing reward within minutes, so editors edit for fun. Long projects don't, so a manager must add reinforcement along the way. Behavior follows what happens after it, not before. What motivates is what someone lacks: praise moves a person starved of it and does little for someone flooded with it. Money is the general reinforcer because we learned it buys anything. Learn each person's history.

### Instruction vagueness should match skill (vid: s6tkRztZwYc — "How to Articulate Your Thoughts")

When training fails, you assumed a skill they didn't have. Low-skill roles leave no room for vagueness, so chunk it down to the smallest action. Senior people can get one line: he can tell a top exec to go market it and trust the rest. Decide the base skills you require at hire, and weigh return on effort before teaching a big gap.

### Two bills from being too far away (extends know where the bodies are buried) (vid: tdLLGKoBojo — "I Lost $100 Million")

An HR director denied a raise ordered a cost-of-living study, then emailed everyone a raise: $600K a year in profit, with one of the biggest to herself. Separately, "delegated" compliance became $120K a month of lawyers reviewing every ad and training and sending notes nobody read, which he puts at $1.2M; a new CFO asked if they were in a lawsuit. Both ran because no one signing checks looked one layer down.

### Today vs the future is a split to manage, not solve (vid: zNiXk_3C_Io — "REAL Reason Not Making Money")

He spent weeks on a big new project until Leila told him the business needed him back, with recording sessions barely happening and him unprepared. A visionary naturally lives months or years out. It never gets solved; it's only the share of the pie you allocate to now versus later. Know which way you lean and overweight the other, because if today fails the future never comes.
